# CHAPTER 6: Generational Fractures & Toward an Adaptive Social Contract

### 6.1 Deconstructing the Friction Points

Chapters 4 and 5 set out the structural pressures acting on Koreans now in their twenties and thirties: a labor market that penalizes career interruption, a housing threshold that has to be cleared before a household can be formed, and a private-education norm that prices parenthood long before a child reaches university. This chapter turns from those pressures to the argument about them — how young men and young women describe the burdens they carry, why the two descriptions so rarely meet, and what institutional adaptation would have to involve if the argument is to become negotiable rather than merely loud.

The Asymmetry of Military Service and Career Entry: For young men, mandatory military service (18 months in the Army and Marine Corps, 20 months in the Navy, and 21 months in the Air Force and in alternative social service) interrupts their early twenties, delaying university graduation and formal labor market entry. In an economy where entry-level competition is severe, many young men read this as a structural disadvantage, and that reading is sharpest when they compare themselves with female peers who enter the labor market without interruption. Survey evidence shows how unevenly the same obligation is understood. In a 2019 Korean Women's Development Institute survey of 3,000 men aged 19 to 59, 72.2 percent of those in their twenties agreed that conscripting only men amounts to discrimination. In Hankook Research's gender-perception surveys, 68 percent of men in their twenties and 75 percent of men in their thirties said military service is not adequately compensated by society, compared with 25 percent and 32 percent of women in the same age brackets. What makes the issue politically combustible is less the length of service than this gap in perception.

The delay itself has a measurable magnitude, even if not quite the one usually invoked. In the May 2026 youth supplement to the Economically Active Population Survey — published by the Ministry of Data and Statistics, the national statistical agency reorganized under that name in October 2025 — the average time from university entry to graduation was 5 years 2.1 months for men and 3 years 11.6 months for women, a gap of roughly fourteen and a half months. Graduates then wait a further 11.3 months on average before their first paid job. The delay is real, in other words, but shorter than the nominal length of service, and it has been narrowing rather than widening: in the same survey, women's time-to-graduate lengthened faster year over year than men's did.

The Risk of Career Interruption: For young women, heavy educational investment tends to produce a professional identity built around that investment. The risk to it is a measured phenomenon rather than an impression. Chapter 4 set out both the official definition of career interruption — non-employed married women aged 15 to 54 who left or changed jobs because of marriage, pregnancy and childbirth, child care, a child's education, or family care — and the reason its measurement is contested, since a survey that leaves the reason open produces a markedly different picture from one built around those categories. In Korean the women concerned are called Gyeongdan-nyeo (경단녀, career-interrupted women), shortened in everyday speech to Gyeongdan. A widely cited reading, and the one this book adopts, is that watching older female cohorts pass through this sequence, together with a disproportionate share of domestic care, encourages caution toward entering conventional family arrangements. The causal claim should be stated carefully: marriage timing is also shaped by housing costs, employment insecurity, and the cost of raising children, which Chapter 5 treats as the primary channel.

The Clash of Unfulfilled Expectations: Young men frequently report pressure to fulfill traditional male roles, above all that of providing the primary housing capital for marriage. Chapter 5 gives the figures — in a 2026 survey of 1,000 recently married couples by the matchmaking company Duo, grooms bore 59.4 percent of total wedding costs and brides 40.6 percent, with housing accounting for 84.5 percent of the total — along with the caution that the sample is drawn from a commercial matchmaking clientele rather than a nationally representative frame. What that evidence does not show is any evening-out of the burden: the academic study Chapter 5 sets alongside it finds the gendered division of marriage capital, the Honsu (혼수) convention included, reproduced rather than resolved. Young women, meanwhile, report being expected to maintain high professional standards while continuing to manage the domestic sphere. This book reads the resulting mismatch as a form of the cultural lag described in the Introduction: the obligations attached to older gender roles have outlasted the social deference that once accompanied them. The mismatch coincides with, and helps explain, unusually high levels of reported friction — in a 2022 Hankook Research survey, 90 percent of respondents in their twenties described gender conflict in Korea as serious, 15 percentage points higher than the previous year. Whether the structural mismatch *produces* that tension is a causal question survey data alone cannot settle.

### 6.2 The Intergenerational Perception Gap: The Industrial Generation and the MZ Cohorts

The debate over family and gender is further complicated by a generational divide. Older cohorts who built South Korea's rapid post-war economic growth tend to evaluate family life through a lens shaped by mid-twentieth-century hardship, whereas younger urban generations more often prioritize personal autonomy, transparent merit, and mental well-being. Korean public discourse groups the latter under the label "MZ generation" (MZ세대), a Korean media coinage bundling Millennials and Generation Z into a single cohort. Readers outside Korea should note that this grouping is rarely used elsewhere: international demography normally treats Millennials (born roughly 1981–1996) and Gen Z (born roughly 1997–2012) as distinct cohorts with different formative conditions. The term is used here because it structures the Korean debate, not because it is a standard analytical category. Generational descriptions of this kind are also generalizations; they characterize tendencies, not individuals.

The Disconnect in Social Norms

Communication Breakdown (Kkondae Discourse): Older family members often frame questions about marital status, childbearing, or career plans as expressions of relational care and warmth — Jeong (정, 情), the dense affective bond of obligation and attachment that Koreans describe as accumulating between people who are close. Younger generations frequently interpret the same inquiries as boundary violations, unwarranted surveillance, and outdated social pressure, and the word Kkondae (꼰대), a slang term for a condescending, lecturing elder that entered mainstream Korean usage around 2019, has become the shorthand for that reading.

The gap shows up in behavior, not only in complaint. In a Hankook Research survey of 1,000 adults conducted before the 2026 Lunar New Year, 54 percent of respondents aged 30 and under said they would spend the holiday quietly at home rather than visit relatives who live apart — the only age group in which that answer was a majority — and only 35 percent of all respondents planned to hold ancestral rites (Charye, 차례) at all, the rites whose gendered division of labor Chapter 1 described. Married respondents were far more likely to plan a family visit than unmarried ones, 79 percent against 56 percent, which suggests the divide may track marital status as much as generation: for an unmarried adult, the holiday gathering doubles as an occasion to be questioned about marriage and career plans.

A separate 2024 promotional survey of 625 adults in their twenties through forties, conducted by the private education company Eduwill, found that the single most commonly cited source of holiday stress was unsolicited interference and prying questions from family and relatives, named by 53.3 percent of respondents, ahead of the labor of preparing holiday food (38.7 percent) and the cost of gifts and cash (36.9 percent). As a commercially run promotional survey rather than a probability sample, its figures should be read as suggestive rather than nationally representative.

Re-Evaluating Sacrifice: This book interprets the generational shift less as a decline of family feeling than as a change in the perceived rationality of sacrifice. The industrial generation was more likely to treat individual sacrifice for the family as a high moral virtue, in a period when such sacrifice was visibly repaid by rising household prosperity. Younger Koreans, facing severe labor-market competition and high housing costs, are more likely to weigh uncompensated sacrifice against the security it actually delivers.

Two large surveys give the contrast an empirical shape. In Statistics Korea's 2024 Social Survey, which polled roughly 36,000 people aged 13 and over, 72.3 percent of respondents aged 60 and over said people should marry, against 39.7 percent of those in their twenties — a gap of more than thirty percentage points. The overall share agreeing that people should marry rose to 52.5 percent, up from 50.0 percent in 2022, a detail worth holding alongside the marriage rebound discussed in 6.3. Pew Research Center's 2023 survey of five East Asian societies found a similar shape on childbearing: about half of South Koreans aged 55 and over said women have an obligation to society to have children, against 8 percent of those aged 18 to 34. These figures measure the distribution of stated norms across age groups, not the reasoning behind them, and a single cross-sectional survey cannot distinguish a genuine generational shift from an ordinary life-cycle effect in which today's twenty-somethings might answer differently once they are older. The interpretation offered here — that what changed is the perceived rationality of sacrifice rather than the depth of family feeling — remains this book's analytical reading rather than a measured finding.

### 6.3 Expanding the Domestic Sphere: De Facto Unions, Single Parenthood, and Civil Partnerships

Marriage rates in South Korea fell sharply over three decades — the crude marriage rate dropped from 9.6 per 1,000 people in 1990 into the three-to-four range through the 2010s — but the most recent data are not a continuation of that slide. According to Statistics Korea's 2024 marriage and divorce statistics, there were 222,000 marriages in 2024, an increase of 29,000, or 14.8 percent, over the previous year. That is the largest absolute increase since 1996 and the largest rate of increase since the series began in 1970, lifting the crude marriage rate to 4.4 and producing the highest annual total since 2019. Marriages then rose again in 2025, by a further 8.1 percent to 240,326, taking the crude rate to 4.7 — a third consecutive annual increase (Ministry of Data and Statistics, 2025 marriage and divorce statistics, released March 2026). Births moved in the same direction: the finalized 2025 birth statistics, released in August 2026, record 254,300 live births, 6.7 percent above 2024.[2] The long-term shift away from marriage-centered household formation has not reversed, and it is too early to say whether the upturn reflects a durable change or the delayed completion of marriages postponed during the pandemic years. But any account of Korean family change written now must distinguish the structural trend from this recent rebound rather than treat decline as self-evidently ongoing.

What is not in question is that the legal and social definition of "family" is under pressure to expand. Korean citizens are forming cohabitation arrangements of a variety that the marriage-centered definition of the domestic unit does not accommodate.

Institutional Lag and Legal Debates

While non-traditional households are increasingly common in urban centers like Seoul, South Korea's legal and welfare frameworks remain largely anchored to marriage-based family units:

The Framework Act on Healthy Families (건강가정기본법): Article 3 still defines "family" as a basic social unit formed through marriage, blood ties, or adoption, and that definition remains in force today. It is worth being precise about the direction of travel. In 2021 the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family adopted revision of the definition as a policy task in the Fourth Basic Plan for Healthy Families (2021–2025), and bills were introduced in the 21st National Assembly to delete the definitional clause and replace "healthy family" with neutral language such as "family support" and "family policy." Those bills lapsed amid organized opposition and a change of governmental position, so the marriage-centered definition was not narrowed but retained. The present tense matters here: the attempt to change the definition failed, which is precisely why it illustrates institutional lag.

The Framework Act sets the tone, but the operative exclusions are produced by specific statutes rather than by the framework law itself, which is largely declaratory. Housing is one example: under the special-supply rules for newlywed couples, eligibility is tied to a registered marriage of no more than seven years as evidenced by the marriage relation certificate, which excludes unmarried cohabiting couples outright. End-of-life and emergency medical decisions are another: the Act on Life-Sustaining Treatment Decisions limits "patient's family" to a spouse aged 19 or over and direct ascendants and descendants within one degree of kinship (extending to two degrees, and then to siblings, only in their absence), a definition that tracks the Civil Act's Article 779 concept of family and leaves an unmarried partner without standing. Keeping these levels distinct — framework declaration, operative statute, administrative rule — matters for the argument this chapter makes in 6.5.

Health insurance is the instructive exception, and it points in the opposite direction from the other two. Dependent registration under the National Health Insurance system has recognized de facto (common-law) opposite-sex spouses, and on 18 July 2024 the Supreme Court, sitting en banc, held by nine votes to four that excluding a same-sex partner from dependent status was unlawful: the National Health Insurance Act contains no express provision excluding same-sex partners, and exclusion on that ground alone constitutes discrimination based on sexual orientation contrary to the constitutional principle of equality. The National Health Insurance Service subsequently registered the plaintiff as his partner's dependent, and the National Human Rights Commission issued a statement welcoming the decision. The pattern echoes the 2005 Constitutional Court decision on the Hoju system discussed in Chapter 3: in Korean family law, judicial reasoning has repeatedly opened the door before the legislature has walked through it.

The Living Together Guarantee Act Movements (Civil Partnership Debates): Legislative proposals inspired by civil pact frameworks have been introduced in the National Assembly. The model most often invoked is France's PACS (Pacte civil de solidarité), introduced in 1999, which allows opposite-sex and same-sex couples to register at the town hall and acquire mutual support obligations along with inheritance, tax, social-security, and labor-related entitlements. The decisive fact about the Korean proposals is that none has ever passed.

Representative Yong Hye-in of the Basic Income Party filed the first Living Together Partnership bill ever formally introduced in the National Assembly in April 2023, with eleven co-sponsoring members; Representative Jang Hye-young of the Justice Party introduced a separate package of three family-formation-rights bills the same year. Both lapsed without deliberation when the 21st Assembly's term expired in May 2024. A new bill was reintroduced by Representative Yong on 3 September 2025 in the 22nd Assembly, extending rights and duties comparable to those of marriage to adults who agree to share and support each other's lives, and it remains pending. Because the bill does not condition eligibility on the partners' sexes, it would cover same-sex couples. Beyond that, this book describes the proposals as extending rights broadly analogous to those currently bundled into marriage, rather than enumerating specific entitlements, because several commonly cited effects would require parallel amendments to tax and medical legislation.

As of this writing (status checked 29 August 2026), the bill was referred to the National Assembly's Legislation and Judiciary Committee the day after it was filed and appears to remain pending there without having reached a committee vote or a floor vote; bill status can change without much public notice, and this should be reconfirmed immediately before publication. One structural feature can be stated with more confidence than the rest: the relationship the bill describes would be created by a jointly signed notification filed with a family court, not by cohabitation alone. That distinction matters for the automatic-versus-registered-rights question taken up in 6.6, and it also answers a claim circulating online that the bill would expose ordinary unmarried cohabitants to property division on breakup, which does not follow from a filing requirement. Whether the bill grants inheritance rights, and whether the tax and insurance effects reported in the press are provisions of the bill itself or amendments that would still need to be made to separate tax and insurance statutes, could not be confirmed from the sources available for this book and should not be asserted either way until the filed text has been checked clause by clause.

### 6.4 Reading the Transition: Adaptation Rather Than Decay

As South Korea navigates the 2020s and beyond, the central sociological argument of Book 2 comes into focus: low fertility, gender conflict, and the decline of marriage-based household formation are better read as a structural call for institutional adaptation than as evidence of moral decay. The wording matters here. This is not a story of families breaking apart in the conventional sense — the crude divorce rate peaked at 3.4 per 1,000 in 2003 and has declined since, standing at 2.2 in 2023 and 1.8 in 2024, with roughly 91,000 divorces in 2024. What has changed is the size and formation of households, not their stability once formed: single-person households rose from 9.0 percent of all households in 1990 to 35.5 percent in 2023 and 36.1 percent in 2024, a series Chapter 5 sets out in full.[1] Popular accounts that read the situation as hedonistic Westernization — the second of the two reductive narratives the Introduction set aside — have the mechanism backwards.

The Confucian legacy is often credited with giving South Korea an extraordinary foundation of educational commitment, social cohesion, and resilience during its rise from post-war poverty to an advanced industrial democracy. The causal weight of that legacy remains contested in the scholarship, and, as the Introduction argued and Chapters 1 and 2 documented, this book does not treat Confucianism as a static cultural essence that explains outcomes on its own. The point here is narrower: the institutional forms Korea built around family obligation proved highly effective under one set of economic conditions. Sustaining the country under a different set requires updating that institutional operating system to align with democratic values, individual autonomy, and genuine gender equality.

By dismantling lingering structural barriers in the workplace, closing the implementation gap for parental leave analyzed in Chapter 4, expanding legal protections for diverse family structures, and establishing genuine partnership within domestic life, South Korea has the capacity to transform its current transitional friction into a more sustainable, inclusive, and resilient future. The three sections that follow set out what that would require in practice: how gender conflict can be reframed as a question of institutional bargaining rather than a contest between camps (6.5), what any legal recognition of diverse families would actually have to specify (6.6), and by what standard the resulting policies should be judged (6.7).

### 6.5 From Gender Conflict to Institutional Bargaining

Public discussion often describes young men and young women as opposing camps. A more precise analysis treats gender conflict as a dispute over the allocation of risk, recognition, and obligation. Men may describe military service, housing costs, and expectations of economic provision. Women may describe safety, career interruption, unpaid care, and the expectation to manage family logistics. These concerns are not identical, and neither side's account should be used to erase the other. They become politically explosive when institutions distribute the costs of adulthood through gendered assumptions.

The task is not to produce a symmetrical equation in which every disadvantage is declared equal. It is to identify where disadvantages originate, who bears them, and which policy can change them. Military service is a state obligation; housing scarcity is a market and policy problem; career interruption is shaped by workplace design and care provision; domestic inequality is negotiated within households but reinforced by wider institutions. Keeping these levels separate makes constructive bargaining more possible.

### 6.6 Legal Pluralism and the Test of Inclusion

Expanding recognition of family diversity requires more than adding a new label to a form. It requires decisions about inheritance, taxation, housing, medical consent, social insurance, parental status, domestic violence protection, and the termination of a relationship. Marriage currently bundles many of these rights into one institution. A civil partnership or cohabitation framework would need to specify which rights are automatic, which require registration, and how vulnerable partners are protected when a relationship ends.

The policy debate should also avoid a false choice between “traditional family” and unlimited individual choice. Many people want durable care relationships, but they may not be able or willing to enter marriage. A pluralistic framework can protect stability without prescribing one household form. The relevant standard is whether people who share care, residence, economic responsibility, or dependency can obtain fair protection, while children and vulnerable adults receive priority.

### 6.7 A Policy Evaluation Framework

The final question is not whether Korea can return to an earlier family model. It is whether institutions can make care, work, and belonging compatible with democratic equality. Four tests are useful. The first is access: can people obtain support regardless of marital status, gender, region, or employment type? The second is time: do policies give adults enough predictable time to care? The third is security: do family choices threaten housing, income, health insurance, or later-life protection? The fourth is voice: can individuals contest decisions made by employers, relatives, welfare agencies, and courts?

These tests produce a more realistic social contract than a promise to restore fertility through incentives alone. This book's assessment is that birth subsidies may help at the margin but cannot compensate for insecure work, unaffordable housing, unequal care, or social stigma. The judgment should be stated as a judgment, because the evidence is genuinely contested: several Korean research institutes have found the fertility effects of cash transfers to be limited, while officials at Statistics Korea have cited government marriage-promotion policy as one factor behind the marriage rebound described in 6.3. That rebound is recent enough that its causes cannot yet be attributed with confidence, and readers should treat both the skeptical and the favorable reading of incentive policy as open.

The two readings can be reconciled more than they first appear to contradict each other. A 2025 study by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs, using twenty-three waves of the Korean Labor and Income Panel Study covering 2000 to 2022, found that birth grants had a statistically significant but very small effect on childbearing relative to other factors, and that at the first- and second-child stages the grants did not move the decision at all; housing and employment security did more of that work. Its policy conclusion favored housing support for newlyweds and youth-employment programs over short-term cash allowances. Lee Chul-hee's 2018 decomposition of Korean fertility between 2000 and 2016 offers the favorable counterpoint, though a narrower one than it is sometimes quoted as: marital fertility rose sharply after pronatalist policy intensified from 2005, and he estimates that without that rise, the total fertility rate in 2016 would have fallen to roughly 0.73 rather than the observed 1.17 — the gain having been offset by the collapsing share of women who were married at all.

Read together, the two studies point less to a flat contradiction than to a difference in where policy has leverage. Cash incentives appear to have moved the fertility of already-married couples somewhat, particularly above roughly six million won per grant (on the order of USD 4,500) according to later threshold estimates, but did nothing to stop the retreat from marriage itself, which Chapter 5 traces to housing and employment insecurity. The larger cash transfers Korea has rolled out since 2022 — the parental benefit and the 2-million-won "first-meeting voucher" (roughly USD 1,500 at the same rate) paid to every newborn household — are too recent for this literature to have evaluated; the population data needed to assess them only became available in late 2024. What is beyond dispute is the objective: a society in which people are not required to choose between meaningful relationships and economic survival.
