# CHAPTER 3: The Hoju System & The Architecture of Legal Transformation

## 3.1 Writing the Household Head into Republican Law

The register assembled by the colonial state between 1909 and 1939, described in Chapter 2, outlived the state that built it. When the Republic of Korea came to draft a civil code of its own in the 1950s, the question was not whether Korea would have its own family law but whether the household-head apparatus inherited from that register would be carried into the law of a constitutional democracy.

During the legislative drafting process, a fierce ideological battle erupted between reformers—among them Lee Tai-young, Korea's first female attorney—and traditionalist Confucian elites (Yurim, 유림). Women's organizations and reform-minded jurists argued that a modern democratic republic founded on constitutional principles of human dignity and gender equality could not adopt feudal family hierarchies. Conservative lawmakers insisted, conversely, that preserving the patriarchal family line was essential to maintaining social order amid post-war instability.

The resulting Civil Act (민법, Act No. 471), promulgated on 22 February 1958 and in force from 1 January 1960, was a deeply compromised framework. While it recognized certain basic civil rights, it formally codified the Hoju system (호주제, 戶主制) into modern statutory law: not inventing the institution, but writing an administrative apparatus inherited from the colonial register into the statute book of a republic. Under this regime, the Hoju (Head of Family) served as the primary legal node through which all family members were registered on the state's household civil-status register (Hojeok, 호적).

## 3.2 Structural Contradictions and Everyday Violence of the Hoju Hierarchy

The statutory mechanics of the Hoju system institutionalized gender discrimination and legal absurdity through Articles 778, 781, 984, and related provisions of the Civil Act.

**The primacy of male infancy over adult female guardianship.** The statutory line of Hoju succession placed biological sex ahead of generation, age, and actual domestic guardianship. When a household head died, a newborn male infant immediately superseded his own adult mother, grandmother, or older sisters to become the legal Head of Family. An adult mother raising her children as the household's sole earner remained legally classified as a member of a household headed, in name, by her minor son.

**The strict paternal surname principle (bu-gye seong-ssi).** Children were legally required to take their biological father's surname and ancestral seat (bon-gwan). Article 781 provided no relief in cases of paternal abandonment, severe domestic abuse, or divorce.

**Administrative stigmatization of blended and single-mother families.** The Hoju registry was compiled as a single, family-wide document. Whenever an individual requested a certified copy (Hojeok Deungbon, 호적등본)—for a passport application, for instance, or for any transaction requiring proof of family relationship—the document exposed the marital and divorce history of everyone recorded on it.

If a divorced mother remarried and brought her biological children into a new household, the children were legally barred from taking their stepfather's surname and remained entered on their father's register. On the register of her new household, the mother herself could appear only through the category of "cohabitant" (donggeo-in, 동거인) rather than as a parent to her own children—a classification that carried real social stigma and bureaucratic friction in schools and workplaces.

## 3.3 Hollowing Out the Hoju: The 1990 Amendment and the 2005 Constitutional Decision

**The 1990 amendment: equality around the head of the family.** Long before the courts intervened, the legislature had already stripped the Hoju of much of its practical substance. The 1990 amendment to the Civil Act (Act No. 4199, passed by the National Assembly on 19 December 1989, promulgated on 13 January 1990, and in force from 1 January 1991) introduced the right to claim division of marital property upon divorce, and placed parental authority (Chin-gwon) and post-divorce custody on a gender-neutral footing anchored in economic contribution and the welfare of the child. It extended legal recognition to maternal-line kin and abolished discrimination in inheritance between sons and daughters, and between children born inside and outside marriage. The same amendment converted household headship from an inheritance-based institution (호주상속) into the weaker "succession to headship" (호주승계), and permitted the eldest son to establish a separate household.

The 1990 amendment therefore hollowed out the Hoju without removing it. By 1991, Korean family law granted extensive equality around the head of the family while still insisting that every family have one. That unresolved contradiction is what the following decade of litigation attacked.

The campaign to dismantle the Hoju system became one of the most sustained civil-rights efforts of South Korea's democratic era. Beginning in the late 1990s, a broad coalition of feminist organizations (such as the Korea Women's Associations United), human-rights lawyers (Minbyun, 민변), and progressive civic groups united under the Citizens' Alliance for the Abolition of the Hoju System (호주제폐지를 위한 시민연대), formed in 2000.

**The constitutional battle.** Activists bypassed stalled legislative lobbying and pursued strategic litigation, mounting multiple constitutional challenges against Civil Act Article 778 (the definition of the Hoju), the latter part of the main text of Article 781(1) (a child enters the father's family register), and the main text of Article 826(3) (a wife enters her husband's family register).

Conservative Confucian groups (Yurim) organized mass street demonstrations, arguing that abolishing the Hoju would destroy family morality, dishonor ancestors, and plunge the nation into moral chaos. Feminist legal scholars countered on constitutional ground, arguing that the system violated Article 10 (individual dignity and human rights) and Article 36, paragraph 1 (gender equality in marriage and family life) of the 1987 Constitution.

**The decision of 3 February 2005 (2001Hun-Ga9 et al.).** On 3 February 2005 the Constitutional Court of Korea delivered a historic ruling of non-conformity to the Constitution (헌법불합치, Heonbeop-bul-hapchi) against three provisions at the core of the Hoju system: Article 778, the latter part of the main text of Article 781(1), and the main text of Article 826(3).

The Court held that assigning family status and authority strictly on the basis of biological sex was an unconstitutional violation of individual dignity. It also acknowledged explicitly that Korean families had diversified into single-parent and blended configurations, and that enforcing a unilateral agnatic hierarchy was anachronistic and unconstitutional.

**Why non-conformity rather than invalidation.** The Court did not strike the provisions down outright. Its order directed that the unconstitutional provisions "shall continue to apply until the legislature amends the Household Registration Act." Immediate invalidation would have left the state with no functioning record for publicly certifying civil status, since the entire register was organized around the household head. This provisional-application order is the reason abolition on paper in 2005 became abolition in fact only on 1 January 2008.

Following the ruling, the National Assembly passed the Civil Act amendment abolishing the Hoju provisions on 2 March 2005. The amended Civil Act (Act No. 7427) was promulgated on 31 March 2005 but—apart from a few provisions—took effect only on 1 January 2008, after a three-year transition during which the challenged provisions remained in force under the Court's order.

One element of the old regime survived the February ruling. The paternal-surname rule itself—the first clause of Article 781(1), "a child shall take the surname and ancestral seat of the father"—was not part of the February case. The Court reached it separately on 22 December 2005 (2003Hun-Ga5·6), again by a decision of non-conformity. The dismantling of patrilineal family law therefore proceeded in three steps: the decision of 3 February 2005 on the Hoju provisions, the Civil Act amendment of March 2005, and the decision of 22 December 2005 on the paternal-surname rule.

## 3.4 The 2008 Reforms: Institutional Individualization

On 1 January 2008 the traditional Hojeok closed, and civil status came to be recorded for each person individually under the Act on Registration of Family Relations (가족관계의 등록 등에 관한 법률, Act No. 8435). Two instruments took effect together on that date: the amended Civil Act described above, which deleted the Hoju provisions themselves, and the registration act, which replaced the household register with a record kept per person. Together they completed the institutional transition from patriarchal household registration to individualized civil-status registration.

**The five individual certificates.** To eliminate discriminatory disclosure, the 2008 system replaced the omnibus family register with five separate, purpose-limited certificates.

Family Relation Certificate (가족관계증명서): records only three direct generational tiers—biological or adoptive parents, spouse, and children—and excludes siblings and ancestral lineage.

Basic Certificate (기본증명서): documents an individual's birth, death, changes of legal status, and nationality.

Marriage Relation Certificate (혼인관계증명서): records marital status and divorce history for the individual only.

Adoption Relation Certificate (입양관계증명서): documents ordinary legal adoption, and the dissolution of an adoption.

Special Adoption Certificate (친양자입양관계증명서): the most tightly restricted of the five. Full adoption grants the adopted child the exact legal status of biological offspring while sealing the record of the birth relationship to prevent social discrimination, and Article 14(2) of the Act limits issuance of this certificate to enumerated circumstances—chiefly a request by the adopted person once he or she has reached adulthood, by prospective spouses establishing whether the consanguinity bars of Civil Act Article 809 apply to them, or on a court's request for factual inquiry or a documented request from investigating authorities.

**Changes in family law.** Under the revised Civil Act Article 781(1), parents may agree at the time of marriage registration to pass the mother's surname and ancestral seat to their children—the legislative answer to the December 2005 decision on the paternal-surname rule.

A father, a mother, or the child may also petition the court under Article 781(6) to change the child's surname and ancestral seat where the change serves the welfare of the child. This is the route by which children in remarried households can now take the surname of the family they actually live in. (A stepfather is not himself named among the petitioners; to become the child's legal parent he must instead go through the full adoption described above.)

Gender equality in divorce settlements—the right to claim division of marital property, and gender-neutral rules on custody and parental authority—did not begin here. Those reforms date from the 1990 amendment described above and had been in force since 1991. What 2008 added was the registration architecture, and the surname rules, that the earlier reforms had left untouched.

The legal deconstruction of the Hoju system was a major democratic achievement, and it erased patriarchal headship from statutory law. It did not, by itself, dislodge decades of entrenched corporate practice or the household division of labor. As South Korean women achieved full legal equality and unprecedented educational attainment, they collided directly with rigid workplace cultures and intensive domestic expectations—the structural dynamic examined in Chapter 4.

## 3.5 What legal individualization changed—and what it did not

The abolition of the Hoju system was a major institutional shift because it changed the state's basic unit of family registration. A person no longer needed to appear as a subordinate member of a legally headed household in order to have civil status. The new system also made it possible to limit the disclosure of sensitive family information by separating certificates according to purpose. These changes mattered in schools, workplaces, courts, and everyday interactions with public administration.

Legal individualization did not, however, make all family relationships equal overnight. Surname practices, inheritance expectations, custody disputes, welfare eligibility, and social judgments continued to be shaped by older norms. Nor should the five certificates be described as a complete privacy regime: each certificate still discloses legally relevant information, and access rules vary according to the applicant and purpose. The important point is institutional direction. The register moved from a household-centered architecture toward a person-centered architecture, while social life remained relational and often unequal.
