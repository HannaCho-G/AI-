## Tone and audience

International, English-language, serious nonfiction readers: students, researchers, policy-interested readers, and deeply engaged Korea/Hallyu followers who want analytical depth rather than a casual travel-culture read (per `00-brief.md`, readership section). The cover should read as **credible and analytical first, inviting second** — closer to a serious Economist/Foreign Affairs-style nonfiction cover than to a lifestyle or travel-guide cover.

## Established visual palette (carry over from interior figures, per 05-visuals.md)

The interior figures in this book all share one deliberate palette, established first in the Introduction's conceptual-map figure and reused consistently through Chapter 6. The cover should use the same system so the book reads as one coherent object:

- **Background:** deep charcoal or dark indigo (not black, not navy-bright — a muted, editorial dark tone)
- **Accent colors (use two or three, not all four, to keep the cover from feeling like an infographic):** deep crimson red, warm ochre/gold, muted teal, slate blue
- **What to avoid:** flat pastel "AI-assistant" minimalism, gaudy neon/gradient looks, glossy 3D bevels, glow effects, generic stock-photo hanbok/palace imagery, or anything that reduces the book to a travel/tourism aesthetic

## Reference style

National Geographic / The Economist / Monocle editorial nonfiction cover design: bold typographic hierarchy, restrained use of a single strong visual motif rather than a busy collage, confident color blocking rather than photographic collage. Think "serious explanatory journalism book," not "K-culture lifestyle guide."

## Recommended image direction (concept only — not generated yet)

Two directions are consistent with the book's own analytical framework and its interior iconography; either could work, and the choice is the author's/Publisher's:

1. **Abstract conceptual motif, echoing the Introduction's four-circle diagram (`intro-img01`) or Chapter 5's concentric-rings motif (`ch5-img03`).** A single bold, abstract geometric motif — for example, two or three interlocking or overlapping color-blocked forms suggesting tension between tradition and modernity, or a family-tree-like branching form rendered in the accent palette rather than literally. This keeps the cover consistent with the book's own visual grammar and avoids the risk of a generated human figure looking inaccurate or generic.
2. **A single strong typographic cover with a restrained institutional motif** (e.g., an abstracted registry/ledger-line pattern echoing the Hoju-to-individual-registration argument of Chapter 3, rendered as pure abstract geometry, no readable text or real document reproduced). This is the lower-risk option since it involves no human figures and no risk of misrepresenting a real person, artifact, or place.

**What the cover should NOT include:** no realistic depiction of any real, named person discussed in the book (e.g., Lee Tai-young, Jeon Tae-il, Yi I/Yulgok, Shin Saimdang) — the same rule applied throughout the interior figures in `05-visuals.md`; no reproduction of a real historical document (e.g., the 1566 Hwahoe Mungi, the Seonghwabo) without a confirmed archival license; no generic "hanbok woman" or "palace gate" stock imagery, which would misrepresent the book's contemporary sociological/legal focus.

> An illustrated analytical ebook for international readers. This book challenges two popular but oversimplified explanations for South Korea's demographic crisis, gender tensions, and family change — "static Confucian determinism" and "hedonistic Westernization" — and replaces them with a documented account of compressed modernization and cultural lag, tracing the Korean family from Goryeo-era bilateral kinship through colonial legal codification, the Hoju system, women's mass entry into education and the workforce, and today's record-low fertility rate.
