# CHAPTER 2: Colonial Disruption, War, and Compressed Modernization

A widespread historical misconception presumes that the 20th-century patriarchal legal order was a direct, uninterrupted continuation of Joseon dynastic tradition. In legal reality, the modern administrative framework of household headship was constructed through the imposition of colonial legal mechanisms, most notably the Chosen Civil Affairs Ordinance (1912) and the Chosen Census Registration Ordinance (1923).

The Japanese colonial administration sought to reorganize Korean society into easily surveilled, taxable, and politically compliant administrative units. To accomplish this, the colonial state transplanted key principles of the Meiji Civil Code's Ie (Household) System:

Statutory Head of Household (Hoju): The colonial authorities designated an official legal head for every domestic registry unit (Hoju-jeok). This transformed informal, customary clan structures into a rigid state instrument of administrative surveillance and direct taxation.

Concentration of Legal Authority: The legal Hoju was granted statutory authority over family members, including formal consent rights over marriages, residences, and property transactions of household constituents.

Erosion of Female Civil Autonomy: While women in early and middle Joseon retained informal protections within their localized kinship networks, the colonial legal system codified women as permanent legal dependents—first subordinate to their fathers, then to their husbands, and ultimately to their sons upon widowhood.

Consequently, modern Korean patriarchy was not an ancient relic; it was a modernized, bureaucratized legal apparatus engineered to serve the surveillance and mobilization needs of an industrializing colonial state.

### 2.2 The Korean War and the Rise of Family Survivalism

Following national liberation in 1945, the catastrophic devastation of the Korean War (1950–1953) destroyed the physical, institutional, and communal fabric of the peninsula.

The war produced millions of civilian casualties, displaced families, and created hundreds of thousands of war widows and orphans. Crucially, the post-war South Korean state possessed neither the fiscal revenue nor the administrative infrastructure to provide a basic public safety net.

In this institutional vacuum, survival required a specific sociological orientation: Familial Survivalism (Gajok Saengjon-juui):

The Family as Welfare State: Because public assistance, unemployment insurance, and universal pensions were non-existent, the household unit served as the sole provider of social insurance, healthcare, housing, and elderly care.

Selective Educational Investment: In agrarian and urban working-class households with limited financial capital, families made calculated collective investments. Families routinely pooled their meager resources to finance the higher education of a single promising son—typically the eldest—relying on his future earning power to elevate the entire household out of poverty.

The Rise of the "Sacrificial Sister": This collective investment strategy depended heavily on the unpaid or underpaid labor of female siblings. Eldest daughters frequently abandoned their own schooling to take low-wage manufacturing, service, or domestic jobs, remitting their wages back home to pay for their brothers' university tuition.

### 2.3 Compressed Industrialization and the Urban Male-Breadwinner Construct

Beginning in the early 1960s, the South Korean government launched an aggressive, export-oriented industrialization drive under state-directed developmental capitalism. Over the span of three decades, millions of rural agricultural workers migrated to rapidly expanding industrial metropolises like Seoul, Incheon, Ulsan, and Busan.

This spatial and economic transformation shattered traditional rural village ecosystems and replaced the multi-generational agrarian clan with the Urban Nuclear Family (Haek-gajok).

To sustain relentless industrial growth, the developmental state and large business conglomerates (Chaebols) structured the urban economy around a strict Male-Breadwinner (Namseong Oeboli) Model:

The Corporate Soldier (San-eop Jeonsa): Male workers were integrated into corporate hierarchies requiring total institutional loyalty, 60-to-70-hour workweeks, mandatory after-work drinking gatherings (Hoesik), and absolute subordination to company objectives.

The Modern Housewife (Jeon-eop Jubu): To sustain this intense male workplace commitment, women were institutionalized as full-time domestic managers. The housewife was tasked with reproductive labor: managing household finances, preparing daily nutrition, caring for aging parents-in-law, and overseeing the academic performance of the children.

The High-Rise Apartment Complex: The mass construction of apartment complexes in Seoul during the late 1970s and 1980s (epitomized by developments in Gangnam, Mok-dong, and Sanggye-dong) served as the physical architecture of this new middle-class ideal.

### 2.4 Female Factory Labor (Yeogong) and the Sacrificial Maternal Ethos

While the middle-class ideal celebrated the stay-at-home housewife (Jeon-eop Jubu), the economic engine of South Korea's early industrial takeoff was powered by young, working-class female laborers, known as Yeogong (여공, Factory Girls).

Throughout the 1960s and 1970s, light manufacturing sectors—such as textiles, apparel, footwear, and consumer electronics assembly—relied on the cheap, disciplined labor of young women aged 16 to 24:

Severe Working Conditions: In industrial hubs such as the Guro Industrial Complex and the Peace Market (Pyeonghwa Sijang) in Seoul, young women worked 14 to 16 hours a day in cramped, poorly ventilated attics and factory floors, exposed to toxic dust and chemical fumes.

Institutionalized Gender Wage Discrimination: Female factory workers were paid less than half the wages of their male counterparts. This wage suppression was rationalized by the patriarchal ideology that women were merely "temporary, supplementary earners" whose wages only supplemented family income until marriage.

Marriage-Bar Practices (Toesa Gwon-go): Corporate management enforced informal and formal "marriage bars," firing female workers the moment they married or became pregnant.

The Sacrificial Ethos (Hyeonmo Yangcheo)
Whether as low-wage factory workers in their youth or as devoted domestic homemakers in adulthood, women during the compressed modernization era were systematically subjected to the cultural ideology of Hyeonmo Yangcheo (현모양처, Wise Mother and Good Wife).

Originally an imported modern concept from Meiji Japan, this ethos reframed traditional Neo-Confucian female subordination into a modern patriotic duty: a woman’s primary national service was to sacrifice personal career ambitions to nurture the next generation of productive industrial workers and support the male corporate workforce.

This structural contradiction—between rising educational qualifications and institutionalized domestic dependency—created the legal, social, and cultural battles that erupted at the turn of the 21st century, beginning with the historic movement to dismantle the Hoju System.

## 2.5 From legal codification to lived family survival

Colonial legal codification did not simply manufacture every patriarchal practice from nothing, nor did it leave Joseon custom untouched. It selected, translated, standardized, and administratively reproduced particular forms of household authority. The result was a layered order in which local practice, Confucian vocabulary, Japanese imperial administration, and later South Korean legislation interacted. This is why the modern family register should be read as both a legal instrument and a technology of visibility: it made relationships legible to the state, but it also classified people in ways that could expose hierarchy and stigma.

The Korean War and the developmental state added another layer. In conditions of displacement, scarcity, and weak public welfare, family networks became practical institutions of insurance. A household could pool earnings, house migrants, educate children, and care for older relatives when markets and public programs were incomplete. The same arrangement that protected members from insecurity could also intensify expectations of obedience and sacrifice. Family survivalism was therefore not merely a sentimental attachment to tradition; it was a rational response to institutional scarcity that later became difficult to unwind.

## 2.6 Women’s work and the hidden subsidy of industrialization

The history of industrialization should not be told only through export figures, factory construction, or male corporate careers. It also depended on a gendered allocation of time. Young women entered factories and offices at wages that were often lower than men’s, while families and women absorbed the costs of migration, unsafe work, interrupted education, and unpaid domestic labor. Later, married women’s withdrawal from formal employment helped stabilize households when firms offered long hours and public childcare was limited. Industrial growth thus received a hidden subsidy from work that national accounts and corporate narratives did not fully value.

This pattern also explains why the male-breadwinner model was never a complete description of Korean households. Many families relied on women’s paid employment before marriage, after widowhood, during economic crisis, or through informal and home-based work. The model was an institutional aspiration and a hierarchy of recognition, not a universal demographic fact. A revised account should therefore distinguish between the breadwinner ideal, the income strategies households actually used, and the care work that made both possible.
