# CHAPTER 2: Colonial Disruption, War, and Compressed Modernization

### 2.1 The Legal Construction of Household Headship, 1909–1939

A widespread historical misconception presumes that the 20th-century patriarchal legal order was a direct, uninterrupted continuation of Joseon dynastic tradition. In legal reality, the modern administrative framework of household headship was assembled step by step through Japanese legal intervention, and it took roughly three decades to complete. The sequence matters, because collapsing it into a single date produces exactly the kind of simplification this book is trying to correct.

**1909 — The Household Registration Law (Minjeokbeop, 민적법).** The first version of the modern household-head register predates annexation. Promulgated on 4 March 1909 as Law No. 8 under the Japanese Residency-General, the Minjeokbeop introduced a Japanese-style register to Korea under the name minjeok ("people's register") rather than hojeok ("household register"). The euphemism was deliberate: in Joseon, being entered in a household register was understood primarily as being made liable for taxation and corvée, and avoidance of registration was widespread. The law changed what a register was for. A document that had chiefly recorded who lived where became an abstract public instrument certifying membership in a legally defined house and the status relations inside it, along Meiji Civil Code lines, with the household head as the responsible authority over that house. Because 1909 precedes the 1910 annexation, this stage is properly described as protectorate-era Japanese legal intervention rather than colonial rule.

**1912 — The Joseon Civil Ordinance (Joseon Minsaryeong, 조선민사령).** Ordinance No. 7 of the Government-General was promulgated on 18 March 1912 and took effect on 1 April 1912. It is frequently cited as the moment Japanese family law was imposed on Korea, but the text says close to the opposite. Article 11 provided that matters of legal capacity, kinship, and inheritance for Koreans were to be governed by Korean custom. Japanese civil law applied broadly to property and commercial relations; family and succession were explicitly carved out and left to "custom" as the colonial courts came to define it. The 1912 ordinance therefore did not transplant the Japanese household-head system. What it did was hand the colonial judiciary the power to determine what Korean custom was.

**1921–1923 — Amendment and the Household Registration Ordinance.** The transplant proper came through amendment. Successive revisions in 1921 and 1922 progressively narrowed the customary exception and brought Korean family and succession law under Japanese principles; the decisive revision of the Joseon Civil Ordinance was promulgated on 7 December 1922 and took effect on 1 July 1923. On the same date the Joseon Household Registration Ordinance (Joseon Hojeongnyeong, 조선호적령, Government-General Ordinance No. 154, promulgated 18 December 1922, effective 1 July 1923) replaced the minjeok apparatus with a full household register. That register survived until 31 December 1959, when the Republic of Korea's own Family Register Act took effect on 1 January 1960. It is this 1922–23 layer, not 1912, that marks the substantive arrival of the Japanese household-head system in Korea.

**1939 — Forced assimilation.** A further amendment in 1939 pushed Korean family law into near-identity with Japanese law, introducing the Japanese-style name system (changssi gaemyeong), adoption of heirs from outside the agnatic line (iseong yangja), and the adopted son-in-law (seoyangja) — all institutions that late Joseon lineage rules had forbidden. As discussed below, this amendment also marks the point at which the legal position of widows was narrowed.

What was transplanted, once the amendments were in place, were key principles of the Ie (家) or house system of the Meiji Civil Code, enacted in 1896 for property and 1898 for kinship and succession:

Statutory Head of Household: Every registered household unit (ho, 戶) was headed by a legally designated hoju (호주, 戶主) and recorded in the family register (hojeok, 호적). This converted informal, locally variable kin arrangements into a standardized state instrument of administrative legibility and taxation.

Concentration of Legal Authority: The hoju held statutory powers over household members, including the right to designate their place of residence and the right to consent to a member's departure from the house (geoga, 去家) or entry into it (ipjeok, 入籍). Because a bride's marriage required removal from her natal register and entry into her husband's, this consent power governed marriage in practice even though it was not framed as a marriage veto. Headship itself passed by house succession (hoju sangsok). [NEEDS RESEARCH: whether the hoju also held a general statutory right to consent to household members' property transactions. Women's Civil Status: A Contested Record. It is tempting to say that colonial law simply codified the Confucian norm of "three obediences" — daughter to father, wife to husband, widow to son — and reduced Korean women to permanent legal dependents. The archival record does not support so clean a statement, and stating it that way would confuse a normative discourse with the operative law. Sungyun Lim's study of colonial court records (Enemies of the Lineage: Widows and Customary Rights in Colonial Korea, 1910–1945) finds that courts under the Government-General initially upheld customary rights permitting widows to become household heads in their own right — the category of female household head (yeohoju, 女戶主) existed and was litigated — and that Korean women brought and won suits over separate property. The decisive narrowing came with the 1939 amendment, which curtailed widows' rights without delivering the expansion of daughters' inheritance that had been promised alongside it. What proved most durable was structural rather than doctrinal: this body of colonial civil law was carried into the postwar Civil Act largely intact, which is why a legal architecture assembled between 1909 and 1939 was still being litigated in Korean courts in the 21st century.

Read this way, modern Korean patriarchy is best understood — this is an interpretation, and a contested one in the scholarship — less as an ancient relic transmitted unchanged than as a bureaucratized legal apparatus built in stages to serve the administrative needs of a colonizing and industrializing state. Section 2.5 returns to this claim and qualifies it, because the opposite error, treating colonial law as having invented Korean patriarchy from nothing, is equally misleading.

### 2.2 The Korean War and the Rise of Family Survivalism

Following national liberation in 1945, the catastrophic devastation of the Korean War (1950–1953) destroyed the physical, institutional, and communal fabric of the peninsula.

Estimates of civilian deaths across the peninsula run from roughly one to two million, with South Korean tallies of war-affected persons — killed, wounded, missing, displaced, and separated from family — reaching several million more. As with every quantitative claim in this book, the figure depends on whether the source is counting deaths or all casualties, and on which side of the line the count was made. The war also created hundreds of thousands of war widows and orphans, conventionally estimated at some 300,000 widows and 100,000 orphans. Crucially, the post-war South Korean state possessed neither the fiscal revenue nor the administrative infrastructure to provide a basic public safety net.

In this institutional vacuum, survival required a specific sociological orientation, which this book calls familial survivalism. The term is analytic shorthand rather than a period expression; the established scholarly vocabulary is familism, and the fullest treatment is Chang Kyung-Sup's account of South Korea's familial political economy under compressed modernity.

The Family as Welfare State: Because public assistance, unemployment insurance, and universal pensions were non-existent, the household unit served as the sole provider of social insurance, healthcare, housing, and elderly care.

Selective Educational Investment: In agrarian and urban working-class households with limited financial capital, families made calculated collective investments. Families pooled their meager resources to finance the higher education of a single promising son—typically the eldest—relying on his future earning power to elevate the entire household out of poverty.

The Sacrificing Sister: This collective investment strategy depended heavily on the unpaid or underpaid labor of female siblings. Oral histories of the period commonly record eldest daughters leaving school to take low-wage manufacturing, service, or domestic jobs and remitting their wages home to pay their brothers' tuition. The phrase "sacrificing sister" is the author's summary of a pattern, not a period term; contemporaries spoke of yeogong (여공, factory girls) and, dismissively, gongsuni. ### 2.3 Compressed Industrialization and the Urban Male-Breadwinner Construct

The First Five-Year Economic Development Plan began in 1962, and its early emphasis lay in import substitution and basic industry; the decisive turn to export-led growth followed the exchange-rate reform of 1964 and the accompanying export-incentive apparatus of preferential export finance, tariff exemptions, export targets, monthly export promotion meetings, and the founding of KOTRA. Over the three decades that followed, millions of rural agricultural workers migrated to rapidly expanding industrial metropolises like Seoul, Incheon, Ulsan, and Busan. Korea's urban share of population rose from roughly 28 percent in 1960 to roughly 74 percent in 1990.

This spatial and economic transformation shattered traditional rural village ecosystems and replaced the multi-generational agrarian clan with the Urban Nuclear Family (Haek-gajok).

To sustain relentless industrial growth, the developmental state and large business conglomerates (Chaebols) structured the urban economy around a strict male-breadwinner model. The model should be read as an institutional ideal and a hierarchy of recognition rather than a description of how every household actually earned its income — a distinction Section 2.6 develops.

The Salaried Man: Male white-collar workers were integrated into corporate hierarchies requiring total institutional loyalty, working hours routinely exceeding 55 hours a week — among the longest recorded in any industrializing economy — mandatory after-work drinking gatherings (Hoesik), and subordination to company objectives. A note on the "industrial soldier." The slogan San-eop Jeonsa (산업전사, industrial warrior) is often applied loosely to this white-collar world, but its history runs elsewhere and is directly relevant to this chapter's argument. The term was coined in imperial Japan during the Asia-Pacific War to equate mobilized laborers with frontline soldiers, and it was revived in 1970s and 1980s South Korea for production workers and for the construction crews dispatched to the Middle East, celebrated as foreign-currency earners. Its reappearance is a small but precise example of colonial-era vocabulary being carried into developmental-era labor ideology.

The Modern Housewife (Jeon-eop Jubu): To sustain this intense male workplace commitment, women were institutionalized as full-time domestic managers. The housewife was tasked with reproductive labor: managing household finances, preparing daily nutrition, caring for aging parents-in-law, and overseeing the academic performance of the children.

The High-Rise Apartment Complex: The mass construction of apartment complexes in Seoul—Gangnam through the 1970s, Mok-dong from 1983 to 1988, Sanggye-dong from 1985 to 1989—served as the physical architecture of this new middle-class ideal.

### 2.4 Female Factory Labor (Yeogong) and the Sacrificial Maternal Ethos

While the middle-class ideal celebrated the stay-at-home housewife (Jeon-eop Jubu), the economic engine of South Korea's early industrial takeoff was powered by young, working-class female laborers, known as Yeogong (여공, Factory Girls).

Throughout the 1960s and 1970s, light manufacturing sectors—such as textiles, apparel, footwear, and consumer electronics assembly—relied on the cheap, disciplined labor of young women, many of them still in their teens. Records of the Guro Industrial Complex describe girls of about fifteen or sixteen arriving from across the country, and the assistants (sida) whose conditions Jeon Tae-il documented at the Peace Market were frequently thirteen to sixteen years old. This was, in substantial part, child labor:

Severe Working Conditions: In industrial hubs such as the Guro Industrial Complex and the Peace Market (Pyeonghwa Sijang) in Seoul, young women worked 14 to 16 hours a day in cramped, poorly ventilated attics and factory floors, exposed to toxic dust and chemical fumes.

Institutionalized Gender Wage Discrimination: Female factory workers were paid less than half the wages of their male counterparts. In 1980 the gender wage gap stood at 55.5 percent—women earning roughly 44 percent of male wages—the widest recorded in any country for which comparable data existed at the time. This wage suppression was rationalized by the patriarchal ideology that women were merely "temporary, supplementary earners" whose wages only supplemented family income until marriage.

Marriage-Retirement and Early-Retirement Rules: Employers operated a marriage-retirement practice (Gyeolhon Toejikje, 결혼퇴직제) and a women's early-retirement-age rule (Jogi Jeongnyeonje, 조기정년제), the latter often set at twenty-five. In practice women were rarely dismissed outright; they were held to pre-signed undertakings or informal expectations and pressed to resign. Women's organizations campaigned against both through the 1980s, contributing to the enactment of the Equal Employment Act in November 1987.

The Sacrificial Ethos (Hyeonmo Yangcheo)
Whether as low-wage factory workers in their youth or as devoted domestic homemakers in adulthood, women during the compressed modernization era were systematically subjected to the cultural ideology of Hyeonmo Yangcheo (현모양처, Wise Mother and Good Wife).

The concept was an import. Its Japanese original, ryosai kenbo (良妻賢母, "good wife, wise mother"), was a Meiji invention institutionalized through the Higher Girls' School Ordinance of 1899, and it entered Korea in the late imperial and early colonial period with its word order reversed—mother placed before wife. Scholars read the inversion as a sign that the Korean patriarchal order absorbing the concept was more rigid than the Japanese one that produced it. The translation is itself a compact illustration of this chapter's larger point: imported institutions were not copied but selected, reordered, and re-anchored in local hierarchy.

The ethos reframed Neo-Confucian female subordination as a modern patriotic duty: a woman's primary national service was to sacrifice personal career ambitions to nurture the next generation of productive industrial workers and support the male corporate workforce. It should be said that the doctrine was double-edged. Because it defined an educated mother as a national asset, it also supplied the leading argument for schooling girls at all, and it opened access to female education that had not previously existed. The same ideology that confined women to the home was the one that first justified sending them to school.

This structural contradiction—between rising educational qualifications and institutionalized domestic dependency—created the legal, social, and cultural battles that erupted at the turn of the 21st century, beginning with the historic movement to dismantle the Hoju System.

## 2.5 From legal codification to lived family survival

Colonial legal codification did not simply manufacture every patriarchal practice from nothing, nor did it leave Joseon custom untouched. It selected, translated, standardized, and administratively reproduced particular forms of household authority. The result was a layered order in which local practice, Confucian vocabulary, Japanese imperial administration, and later South Korean legislation interacted. This is why the modern family register should be read as both a legal instrument and a technology of visibility: it made relationships legible to the state, but it also classified people in ways that could expose hierarchy and stigma.

The Korean War and the developmental state added another layer. In conditions of displacement, scarcity, and weak public welfare, family networks became practical institutions of insurance. A household could pool earnings, house migrants, educate children, and care for older relatives when markets and public programs were incomplete. The same arrangement that protected members from insecurity could also intensify expectations of obedience and sacrifice. Family survivalism was therefore not merely a sentimental attachment to tradition; it was a rational response to institutional scarcity that later became difficult to unwind.

## 2.6 Women’s work and the hidden subsidy of industrialization

The history of industrialization should not be told only through export figures, factory construction, or male corporate careers. It also depended on a gendered allocation of time. Young women entered factories and offices at wages that were often lower than men’s, while families and women absorbed the costs of migration, unsafe work, interrupted education, and unpaid domestic labor. Later, married women’s withdrawal from formal employment helped stabilize households when firms offered long hours and public childcare was limited. Industrial growth thus received a hidden subsidy from work that national accounts and corporate narratives did not fully value.

This pattern also explains why the male-breadwinner model was never a complete description of Korean households. Many families relied on women’s paid employment before marriage, after widowhood, during economic crisis, or through informal and home-based work. The M-shaped female labor force participation curve shown in Figure 7 makes the pattern visible across the life course. The model was an institutional aspiration and a hierarchy of recognition, not a universal demographic fact. A revised account should therefore distinguish between the breadwinner ideal, the income strategies households actually used, and the care work that made both possible.
