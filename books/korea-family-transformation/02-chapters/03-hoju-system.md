# CHAPTER 3: The Hoju System & The Architecture of Legal Transformation

During the legislative drafting process, a fierce ideological battle erupted between progressive female legal scholars—led by Dr. Lee Tai-young (Korea's first female attorney)—and traditionalist Confucian elites (Yurim). Progressive jurists argued that a modern democratic republic founded on constitutional principles of human dignity and gender equality could not adopt feudal family hierarchies.

Conversely, conservative lawmakers insisted that preserving the patriarchal family line was essential to maintaining social order amid post-war instability.

The resulting 1958 Civil Code was a deeply compromised framework. While it recognized certain basic civil rights, it formally codified the Hoju System (호주제, 戶主制) into modern statutory law. Under this regime, the Hoju (Head of Family) served as the primary legal node through which all family members were registered on the municipal household census registry (Hoju-jeok).

### 3.2 Structural Contradictions and Everyday Violence of the Hoju Hierarchy

The statutory mechanics of the Hoju system institutionalized extreme gender discrimination and legal absurdities under Articles 778, 781, 984, and related provisions of the Civil Code.

- The Primacy of Male Infancy Over Adult Female Guardianship
The statutory line of Hoju succession prioritized biological sex over generation, age, and actual domestic guardianship. If a household head died:

A newborn male infant immediately superseded his own adult mother, grandmother, or older sisters to become the legal Head of the Family (Hoju).

An adult mother raising her children as the sole economic earner remained legally classified as a dependent under the nominal headship of her minor son.

- The Strict Paternal Surname Principle (Bu-gye Seong-ssi)
Children were legally mandated to inherit solely their biological father's surname and ancestral seat (Bon-gwan). Under Article 781, no exception was granted—even in cases of paternal abandonment, severe domestic abuse, or divorce.

- Administrative Stigmatization of Blended and Single-Mother Families
The Hoju registry was compiled as a unified family-wide document. Whenever an individual requested a certified copy (Hojeok Deungbon) for school admissions, employment verification, or passport issuance, the document exposed the complete marital and divorce history of all registered members.

If a divorced mother remarried and brought her biological children into a new household, the children were legally barred from taking their stepfather's surname.

On official registers, the children were recorded as "cohabitants" (Donggeo-in) rather than sons or daughters, subjecting blended families and stepchildren to severe societal stigma and bureaucratic discrimination in school and workplace environments.

### 3.3 The Civil Coalition and the Landmark 2005 Constitutional Court Decision

The battle to dismantle the Hoju system was one of the most sophisticated, enduring civil rights campaigns in South Korea's democratic history.

Beginning in the late 1990s, a broad coalition of feminist organizations (such as the Korea Women's Associations United), human rights attorneys (Minbyun), academic sociologists, and progressive civic groups united under the Citizens' Alliance for the Abolition of the Hoju System (2000).

The Constitutional Battle
Activists bypassed stalled legislative lobbying and pursued strategic litigation, filing multiple constitutional complaints challenging Civil Code Article 778 and Article 781 (1).

Conservative Confucian groups (Yurim) organized mass street demonstrations, arguing that abolishing Hoju would destroy family morality, dishonor ancestors, and plunge the nation into moral chaos.

Feminist legal scholars countered with constitutional jurisprudence, demonstrating that the system directly violated Article 10 (Individual Dignity and Human Rights) and Article 36, Paragraph 1 (Gender Equality in Marriage and Family Life) of the 1987 Constitution.

The February 3, 2005 Decision (2001Hun-Ga9 et al.)
On February 3, 2005, the Constitutional Court of Korea delivered a historic ruling of Non-Conformity to the Constitution (헌법불합치) against the core provisions of the Hoju system:

Violation of Human Dignity: The Court held that assigning family status and authority based strictly on biological sex was an unconstitutional violation of individual dignity.

Pluralism of Family Structures: The Court explicitly acknowledged that modern Korean families had diversified into nuclear, single-parent, and blended configurations, and that enforcing a unilateral agnatic hierarchy was anachronistic and unconstitutional.

Following this ruling, the National Assembly passed the Civil Code amendment abolishing the Hoju provisions on March 2, 2005 (the amended Civil Code was promulgated on March 31, 2005).

### 3.4 The 2008 Family Relation Registration Act: Institutional Individualization

On January 1, 2008, the traditional Hojeok (Family Registry) was officially dismantled and replaced by the Act on Registration of Family Relations (가족관계의 등록 등에 관한 법률). This legislative milestone marked the complete institutional transition from patriarchal household registration to individualized civil status registration.

The Five Individual Certificates
To eliminate discriminatory disclosures, the 2008 system replaced the omnibus family register with five separate, targeted individual certificates:

Family Relation Certificate (가족관계증명서): Records only three direct generational tiers: biological/adoptive parents, spouse, and children. (Excludes siblings and ancestral lineage).

Basic Certificate (기본증명서): Documents an individual's personal birth, death, legal status changes, and nationality.

Marriage Relation Certificate (혼인관계증명서): Records marital status and divorce history for the individual only.

Adoption Relation Certificate (입양관계증명서): Documents standard legal adoption records.

Special Adoption Certificate (친양자입양관계증명서): Accessible only under strict judicial authorization, granting adopted children the exact legal status of biological offspring while sealing biological birth records to prevent social discrimination.

Revolutionary Changes in Family Law
Maternal Surname Inheritance: Under revised Civil Code Article 781 (1), parents may agree at the time of marriage registration to pass the mother's surname and ancestral seat to their children.

Surname Change for Stepchildren: Stepfathers or mothers can petition the Family Court to alter a minor child’s surname to match the custodial family unit when it serves the child's psychological welfare.

Judicial Gender Equality: Property division upon divorce, child custody rights, and parental authority (Chin-gwon) were legally re-anchored to gender-neutral principles of economic contribution and child welfare.

Institutional Progress vs. Sociological Inertia
The legal deconstruction of the Hoju system was a monumental democratic achievement that erased patriarchal headship from statutory law. However, legal transformation did not instantly eliminate decades of entrenched corporate practices and domestic division of labor.

As South Korean women achieved full legal equality and unprecedented educational milestones, they collided directly with rigid workplace cultures and intensive domestic expectations—the structural dynamic examined in Chapter 4.

## 3.5 What legal individualization changed—and what it did not

The abolition of the Hoju system was a major institutional shift because it changed the state’s basic unit of family registration. A person no longer needed to appear as a subordinate member of a legally headed household in order to have civil status. The new system also made it possible to limit the disclosure of sensitive family information by separating certificates according to purpose. These changes mattered in schools, workplaces, courts, and everyday interactions with public administration.

Legal individualization did not, however, make all family relationships equal overnight. Surname practices, inheritance expectations, custody disputes, welfare eligibility, and social judgments continued to be shaped by older norms. Nor should the five certificates be described as a complete privacy regime: each certificate still discloses legally relevant information, and access rules vary according to the applicant and purpose. The important point is institutional direction. The register moved from a household-centered architecture toward a person-centered architecture, while social life remained relational and often unequal.
