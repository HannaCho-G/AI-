# CHAPTER 1: The Confucian Foundation & The Construction of Patrilineal Order

Extensive historical, legal, and anthropological research reveals that during the Koryo Dynasty (918–1392) and the early centuries of the Joseon Dynasty (15th to mid-16th centuries), Korean kinship organization was substantially bilateral—giving roughly equal weight and status to both maternal and paternal bloodlines.

- Equal Partible Inheritance (Gyunbun Sangsok)
Under early Joseon legal codes, including the Gyeongguk Daejeon (National Code), property was distributed equally among all siblings. Daughters—even after marriage—retained full property rights over their inherited estates, maintaining independent economic security throughout their adult lives.

A prominent historical illustration is found in the 1566 Property Division Record (Hwahoe Mungi) of the renowned Neo-Confucian scholar Yi I (Yulgok) and his siblings. The seven children of Lady Shin Saimdang (four sons and three daughters) divided their parents' land and assets in precise equal proportions. Yi I, despite his intellectual prominence as the third son, received the exact same share as his married sisters.

- Uxorilocal Residence (Seoryu Buga-hon)
The standard marital residence pattern in early Joseon was Seoryu Buga-hon (literally, "the son-in-law stays at the bride’s home"). Upon marriage, the groom moved into the bride’s household, often remaining there for extended periods—sometimes until their own children reached adulthood.

This practice anchored women firmly within their natal family’s emotional, social, and economic protection, preventing the domestic isolation and extreme subservience that characterized later periods.

- Rotational Ancestral Rites (Yunhoe Bongsa)
Ancestral veneration rites (Jesa) were not the exclusive monopoly of the eldest son. Sons and daughters took turns hosting annual memorial rituals (Yunhoe Bongsa), or divided the ancestral lineages among themselves (Bunhal Bongsa). Furthermore, in the absence of sons, daughters or maternal grandsons (Oeson Bongsa) routinely officiated ancestral rites without social stigma.

### 1.2 The Post-War Crisis and the Orthodoxy of Zhu Xi Neo-Confucianism

This balanced, bilateral kinship arrangement underwent a systemic dismantling during the 17th and 18th centuries. The catalyst for this profound civilizational transformation was the catastrophic disruption caused by successive foreign invasions: The Imjin War (Japanese invasions, 1592–1598) and The Manchu Invasions (1627–1636).

Faced with devastated agricultural production, empty state coffers, and severe erosion of social stratification between the noble elite (Yangban) and commoners, the ruling elite sought to stabilize the state by imposing a rigid, orthodox reading of Zhu Xi Neo-Confucianism.

The elite argued that national disorder stemmed from moral laxity and loose household boundaries. To re-establish cosmic and political equilibrium, they mandated strict adherence to Zhu Xi’s Family Rituals (Jujagarye, 朱子家禮), restructuring the entire social architecture around exclusive agnatic (male-lineage) descent lines.

### 1.3 Agnatic Primogeniture, Clan Genealogy (Jokbo), and Ancestral Monopoly

By the mid-to-late 18th century, the legal and social codes of Joseon had institutionalized a sweeping shift toward Patrilineal Primogeniture (Jeokjangja Supremacy).

- The Consolidation of Estate Wealth
Partible inheritance was increasingly viewed by the elite as an economic liability that fragmented clan landholdings across generations. To preserve the economic and political status of elite lineages, inheritance laws were modified to funnel the vast majority of family wealth (land and ritual assets) directly to the Eldest Son (Jangnam). Second and third sons received marginal shares, while married daughters were excluded from inheritance.

- The Transformation of Clan Genealogies (Jokbo)
Early clan records, such as the famous Seonghwabo (1476) of the Andong Kwon clan, registered children purely according to biological birth order, proudly listing daughters, sons-in-law, and maternal grandchildren alongside sons.

By the 18th century, however, Jokbo registries were radically restructured: sons were listed first in order of seniority, while daughters were relegated to the margins or recorded merely as "the wife of [Son-in-Law’s Name]," structurally erasing female identity from the patrilineal record.

- Agnatic Adoption Restrictions (Gyehu)
Under late Joseon legal mandates, if a household head died without a male heir, the family was legally forbidden from passing ritual leadership or lineage headship to a daughter or maternal grandson. The family was legally required to adopt a male child (Gyebi) exclusively from within the same agnatic clan branch (Dangnae), ensuring the unbroken continuity of the paternal Y-chromosome lineage.

### 1.4 Chulga-oein (The Outsider Norm) and the Gendered Division of Ritual Labor

The institutionalization of patrilineal primogeniture profoundly diminished the social, legal, and relational autonomy of Korean women.

- Patrilocal Exogamy and Chulga-oein
The practice of uxorilocal residence was replaced by strict Patrilocal Residence (Chin-yeong, 親迎). Upon marriage, a woman was formally transferred from her birth family to her husband's household, permanently residing under the authority of her parents-in-law (Sijip-sali).

She was designated as Chulga-oein (出嫁外人)—literally, "a person who has left the household and become an outsider." Stripped of inheritance from her biological parents, her domestic security in her husband's home was entirely conditional upon her ability to produce a male heir (Jeokjangja), institutionalizing extreme social pressure on female reproduction.

- The Ritual Division of Labor
Ancestral veneration rites (Jesa and Charye) formed the core moral and spiritual duty of Confucian family life. However, these sacred rituals functioned through a deeply asymmetric gendered division of labor:

Male Authority: Men officiated the public proceedings, read the ceremonial elegies, bowed before the ancestral tablets, and claimed the spiritual and social prestige of honoring the lineage.

Female Labor: Women, led by the eldest daughter-in-law (Jongbu), were tasked with the exhausting, multi-day physical labor of preparing dozens of highly codified ceremonial food offerings, operating behind the scenes in kitchens and courtyards.

This structural separation—public prestige for men, unpaid domestic labor for women—remained deeply embedded in the Korean domestic consciousness, serving as the cultural root for modern holiday conflicts (Myeongjeol Syndrome) and intergenerational marital tensions well into the 21st century.

### 1.5 Filial Piety (Hyo) as Political Architecture: Isomorphism of Family and State

In the Neo-Confucian governance of Joseon, Filial Piety (Hyo, 孝) was far more than an ethical guideline for personal character; it was the foundational political philosophy of state sovereignty.

- The Isomorphism of Family and State (Chung-Hyo Ilchi)
Neo-Confucian political theory constructed the state as an expanded family and the family as a miniature state:

The absolute authority of the father over the household was framed as a direct reflection of the absolute authority of the King over his subjects.

Devotion to parents (Hyo) served as the primary psychological training ground for unconditional political loyalty (Chung, 忠) to the monarch.

Under the dynastic criminal code (Gyeongguk Daejeon and Daemingnyul), unfilial behavior (Bulhyo) was categorized as one of the Ten Abominations (Sip-ak)—a treasonous crime punishable by severe state penalties or execution.

- Familial Collectivism vs. Modern Individualism
Unlike modern Western legal philosophies that position the autonomous individual with inalienable natural rights as the basic building block of society, traditional Korean culture positioned the Extended Family Lineage (Gammun) as the fundamental social atom:

Shared Collective Responsibility: Personal achievements or failures were never evaluated in isolation; they elevated or disgraced the reputation of the entire lineage.

Intergenerational Reciprocity: Parents were expected to make extreme, sacrificial investments in their children's social advancement. In reciprocal return, adult children were bound by an absolute moral contract to physically, emotionally, and financially support their parents in old age.

This collectivistic ethic provided a resilient survival mechanism through centuries of foreign invasions, poverty, and state collapse. However, when this framework collided with the hyper-meritocratic, urbanized capitalist economy of the late 20th and early 21st centuries, it created acute structural gridlock—setting the stage for the dramatic legal, labor, and demographic transformations examined in the chapters that follow.

## 1.6 Evidence, variation, and the limits of the historical model

The historical argument requires a qualification. Early Joseon records showing daughters’ inheritance, maternal kin connections, or residence near the wife’s family do not demonstrate that gender equality existed in a modern sense. Social rank, household wealth, region, marital status, and the distinction between formal entitlement and actual possession all mattered. Nor did the later consolidation of patrilineal norms occur at one identical moment for every community. The safest interpretation is therefore one of directional transformation: over several centuries, a more flexible kinship field was increasingly organized around agnatic descent, male ritual leadership, and the reproduction of the household line.

This qualification strengthens rather than weakens the argument. It prevents two opposite errors. The first is to project the late Joseon model backward and imagine that all Korean families had always been organized by the same patriarchal rules. The second is to romanticize earlier arrangements as an egalitarian golden age. Historical households were capable of giving daughters property while still arranging marriages, labor, and ritual obligations through gendered hierarchies. The analytical question is not whether one period was simply “free” and another “oppressive,” but which rights, duties, and resources were attached to which relationships.
