# CHAPTER 1: The Confucian Foundation & The Construction of Patrilineal Order

### 1.1 Bilateral Kinship in Goryeo and Early Joseon

Extensive historical, legal, and anthropological research reveals that during the Goryeo Dynasty (918–1392) and the early centuries of the Joseon Dynasty (15th to mid-16th centuries), Korean kinship organization was substantially bilateral—giving roughly equal weight and status to both maternal and paternal bloodlines. This is the standard account in the field; Martina Deuchler's *The Confucian Transformation of Korea: A Study of Society and Ideology* (1992) remains its reference statement, tracing the shift toward agnatic organization through ancestral rites, mourning grades, inheritance, marriage, and the formation of descent groups.

- Partible Inheritance Between Sons and Daughters (Gyunbun Sangsok)

Under early Joseon legal codes, the *Gyeongguk Daejeon* (National Code) prescribed division of property between sons and daughters without discriminating by sex—a provision set out in the Punishments section, in the articles on private slaves. The division was not, however, mathematically equal. The ritual heir, meaning the eldest son who carried on the ancestral rites, received an additional one-fifth share earmarked as the ritual portion (bongsajo) to fund those rites; sacrificial land, ritual implements, and grave land passed to him alone. Children born to concubines received reduced fractions—one-seventh of a legitimate child's share for the children of a commoner concubine, one-tenth for the children of a slave concubine.

The significant fact, then, is not perfect equality but the absence of a gender line: a married daughter was a full party to the division. Daughters retained legal title to their inherited property after marriage, and that property was not absorbed into the husband's lineage estate. How much practical control a woman exercised over such holdings—as against her husband, her in-laws, or her natal family—is a separate question, and one the surviving documents answer far less clearly.

A prominent historical illustration is found in the 1566 Property Division Record (Hwahoe Mungi) of the renowned Neo-Confucian scholar Yi I (Yulgok) and his siblings—a document that survives today as a designated national treasure (Bomul No. 477). Its opening line dates the agreement to the twentieth day of the fifth month of 1566, when the siblings born of the same mother gathered to settle their parents' estate. The seven children of Lady Shin Saimdang (four sons and three daughters) are named in the document in birth order, with Yi I appearing as the third son, then holding office as a junior official in the Ministry of Personnel.

The order of the division matters as much as its outcome. The siblings first set aside a common fund of house, land, and slaves for the memorial rites and the upkeep of the graves. They then allotted a portion to their father's concubine, Lady Kwon. Only the remainder was divided among the seven siblings—and it was divided without discriminating between sons and daughters. The document states explicitly that it followed the rules of the *Gyeongguk Daejeon*, which, as noted above, already built in an extra share for the ritual heir. So Yi I, for all his intellectual prominence, took his place in the division alongside his married sisters rather than above them.

The document survives today in the collection of Konkuk University Museum in Seoul—not, as is sometimes assumed, at Ojukheon in Gangneung, which holds a related but distinct 1548 property-division record left by Yi I's maternal grandmother. (Konkuk University's own treasure designation, registered in 1968, and Korea's National Heritage Portal both confirm the Seoul location; the Gangneung document is a separate artifact, designated as a Gangwon Province Tangible Cultural Heritage in its own right.)

The item-by-item figures survive well enough to test the "no gender line" claim directly. As reconstructed from the document by the Kyunghyang Shinmun's serialized reading of historical artifacts (2019, 2023) and cross-checked against the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture's entry on the document:

| Sibling | Paddy (*majigi*) | Slaves |

| Eldest son, Yi Seon | 15 | 16 |
| Eldest daughter (married to Jo Dae-nam) | 10 | 16 |
| Second son, Yi Beon | 8 | 16 |
| Second daughter (married to Yun Seop) | 8 | 15 |

The slave allotments were almost identical—sixteen or fifteen persons each, a spread of one—and the extra person went to the three eldest children regardless of sex: the eldest daughter was in the larger group, while Yi I himself was in the smaller one. Summed across the seven siblings, the slaves come to at least 108 (a few secondary accounts cite a higher total, apparently through confusion with an unrelated document of similar size, so 108 should be read as a floor rather than a settled count). The paddy allotments varied more widely, from eight to fifteen *majigi*, but again not along a gender line: the third daughter received twelve *majigi* against Yi I's own eight. Two qualifications matter here. A *majigi* is a sowing-capacity measure—the area a person could plant with a set volume of seed grain—not a fixed unit of area, and Joseon land valuation additionally graded fields by fertility under the *gyeolbu* system; unequal *majigi* figures therefore do not by themselves establish unequal value. The record also allotted each sibling a share of dry field (bat), but the surviving summaries of the document report only that a field portion was assigned to everyone, without giving per-person acreage. Read together, the figures support the document's usual reading but sharpen it: sex was not the axis of division, but the division was not arithmetically equal along any single axis either. The one-person gap in slaves tracked birth order among the three eldest children; the wider gap in paddy tracked something closer to land quality and location. What did not track either variable was gender.

- Uxorilocal Residence (Seoryu Buga-hon)

The dominant marital residence pattern in early Joseon was Seoryu Buga-hon (壻留婦家婚, literally "the son-in-law stays at the bride's home"), also known as Namgwi Yeoga-hon. Upon marriage, the groom moved into the bride's household, often remaining there for extended periods—sometimes until their own children were well grown. Lady Shin Saimdang is herself the most familiar illustration: she gave birth to Yi I at her natal home in Gangneung and raised him there.

This practice kept women inside their natal family's emotional, social, and economic orbit, which meant they were less exposed to the domestic isolation later associated with patrilocal residence. That is an interpretation of the arrangement's consequences, not a claim documented household by household.

- Rotational Ancestral Rites (Yunhoe Bongsa)

Ancestral veneration rites (Jesa) were not the exclusive monopoly of the eldest son. Into the first half of the seventeenth century, the duty of maintaining the rites was understood to fall on the descendants as a group rather than on one designated son. Sons and daughters took turns hosting annual memorial rituals (Yunhoe Bongsa), or divided the ancestral lineages among themselves (Bunhal Bongsa). Where there were no sons, daughters or maternal grandsons performed the rites (Oeson Bongsa) as an accepted practice; the custom is documented well enough to hold its own entry in the standard encyclopedias of Korean folk culture. It declined through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as lineage law (jongbeop) hardened.

These three elements belong together. Rotational rites were possible because property was divided among daughters as well as sons, and because a married daughter often remained near, or within, her natal household. Remove the material and residential base and the ritual arrangement loses its footing—which is roughly what happened next.

### 1.2 The Post-War Crisis and the Orthodoxy of Zhu Xi Neo-Confucianism

This balanced, bilateral kinship arrangement underwent a systemic dismantling over the following centuries. The transformation was long in the making—Confucianizing legislation dates from the dynasty's founding in 1392, and the court was already pressing for reformed marriage procedure in the fifteenth century—but it was sharply accelerated by the catastrophic disruption of successive foreign invasions: the Imjin War (Japanese invasions, 1592–1598) and the Manchu invasions of 1627 and 1636–37.

The post-war landscape is conventionally described in terms of collapsed agricultural production, exhausted state finances, and the loosening of the status boundary between the noble elite (Yangban) and commoners—the latter driven in part by fiscal expedients such as grain-contribution ranks (nabsokchaek) and blank appointment certificates (gongmyeongcheop), which put status markers within reach of those who could pay for them. The scale of the collapse can be measured in the state's own land registers. Registered arable land across the eight provinces stood at roughly 1,511,500 *gyeol* before the Japanese invasions, according to the seventeenth-century reformer Yu Hyeong-won's *Bangyesurok* (Book 6). By the eighth month of 1601, three years after the fighting ended, the court itself put the figure at a little over 300,000 *gyeol*—by contemporary reckoning, less than the province of Jeolla alone had carried in peacetime—and the official Yi Hang-bok reported to the throne in the same month that the population stood at barely a tenth of its pre-war level (a contemporary official's impression, not a census figure, and it should be read as such). The first postwar cadastral survey, completed in 1604, brought the registered total back to about 540,000 *gyeol*; the land base did not recover its pre-war level until around 1650, roughly half a century after the Japanese invasions began. A *gyeol* was a unit of assessed yield rather than of area—the same nominal *gyeol* could cover very different acreage depending on soil grade—so the drop also reflects wartime disruption of the survey apparatus and the concealment of land from the tax rolls (eunkyeol), not only fields physically destroyed. Even so, a fiscal state that saw its registered tax base fall by roughly four-fifths within a decade is the state that then turned to selling status through grain-contribution ranks and blank appointment certificates.

The elite argued that national disorder stemmed from moral laxity and loose household boundaries. To re-establish cosmic and political equilibrium, Zhu Xi's *Family Rituals* (Jujagarye, 朱子家禮) was enforced as the orthodox standard for elite household ritual, restructuring the social architecture of the yangban stratum around exclusive agnatic (male-lineage) descent lines. This was less a matter of state statute than of a ritual orthodoxy propagated within scholar-official society itself.

### 1.3 Agnatic Primogeniture, Clan Genealogy (Jokbo), and Ancestral Monopoly

By the mid-to-late eighteenth century, elite inheritance *practice* had shifted decisively toward the eldest son—even though the statutory codes still prescribed division between sons and daughters. This point deserves emphasis, because it is easy to get backwards. Joseon's written law was never amended to install primogeniture. The equal-division principle of the *Gyeongguk Daejeon* was carried forward through the later compilations, down to the nineteenth-century *Daejeon Hoetong*, which restated it. What changed was what families actually did.

The evidence for that change is documentary. Property-division records (bunjaegi) from this period show the ritual portion swelling, younger sons receiving token shares, and married daughters dropping out of the division altogether. The gap between what the law said and what families did is itself the historical evidence—and it is worth registering now, because this book will return again and again to the same structure. A codified rule points one way; household practice runs ahead of it, or lags behind it, by decades or centuries. Eighteenth-century Joseon is the earliest clear instance in this book of a phenomenon that later chapters will meet under the name of cultural lag.

- The Consolidation of Estate Wealth

Partible inheritance was increasingly viewed by the elite as an economic liability that fragmented clan landholdings across generations. Each round of division subdivided a lineage's land base further, eroding the economic footing that sustained a scholar-official family's standing. Concentrating land and ritual assets in the eldest son (Jangnam), and enlarging the ritual portion attached to him, was the practical answer. Second and third sons received marginal shares, and married daughters were, in practice, excluded from the division—not by any statute barring them, but by the accumulated weight of what families had come to regard as normal.

- The Transformation of Clan Genealogies (Jokbo)

Early clan records, such as the famous Seonghwabo (1476) of the Andong Kwon clan—the oldest surviving printed Korean genealogy—registered children according to biological birth order without separating them by sex, listing daughters in their birth position together with sons-in-law and maternal grandchildren. Remarriage was recorded openly, with a woman's former and later husbands both entered, and no distinction was drawn between the children of wives and those of concubines.

One qualification is essential here, and it cuts against a common assumption. Even in 1476, a daughter was ordinarily entered not under her own name but under her husband's, since social convention avoided writing the personal names of women outside the lowest status groups. Recording daughters through their husbands was therefore not an eighteenth-century innovation. It was already the convention.

What actually changed by the eighteenth century was twofold. First, the ordering: entries shifted from birth sequence to sons-first (seonnam-hunyeo). Second, and more consequentially, the scope of maternal-line recording contracted. During the seventeenth century some lineages began capping the registration of daughters' descendants at three generations, and later compilations narrowed further, until little remained on the daughter's side beyond the son-in-law's name. Over the same period the genealogical information recorded about daughters-in-law grew richer. The kinship network was not simply losing its women; its female connecting points were migrating from the daughter to the daughter-in-law. Of all the evidence for the narrowing of Korean kinship into a single agnatic channel, this shift in the genealogies is perhaps the most direct.

- Establishing an Heir (Gyehu)

As lineage norms hardened, a household without a son was expected—not by criminal statute but by the weight of ritual orthodoxy and lineage pressure—to establish an heir by adopting a boy from the same agnatic descent group, rather than passing the rites to a daughter or a daughter's son. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the conviction that the rites must be carried by a son had become general, and adoption displaced maternal-grandson succession as the standard remedy.

The legal instrument was permissive rather than prohibitive. The *Gyeongguk Daejeon*'s provision on establishing an heir (Ipu) allowed a couple without sons by either wife or concubine to install a non-eldest son of a collateral agnate (dongjong ji jaja) as successor. The procedure required registration with the Board of Rites and the consent of the fathers on both sides. In practice, lineages preferred to draw the adoptee from close kin within the mourning circle (Dangnae, conventionally relatives within eight degrees), though that term describes customary preference, not a statutory requirement.

Nothing in this framework outlawed maternal-grandson succession, and late Joseon property records still contain cases in which ritual duty and an accompanying grant of property passed to a daughter's descendants. What the system produced was not a prohibition but a strong default—continuity of the agnatic line of ritual succession, secured by adoption where biology had not obliged. It is worth noting that adoption of this kind was a ritual and legal succession, not a biological one; the adopted heir might share no traceable descent with the man whose rites he assumed.

### 1.4 Chulga-oein (The Outsider Norm) and the Gendered Division of Ritual Labor

The consolidation of patrilineal primogeniture in practice profoundly diminished the social and relational autonomy of Korean women.

- Patrilocal Marriage and Chulga-oein

Uxorilocal residence gave way to patrilocal residence organized around Chin-yeong (親迎), the procedure by which the groom fetches the bride to his own home. This was a long campaign rather than a post-war rupture: the court was already conducting royal weddings by chin-yeong in 1435, and it took centuries for the practice to penetrate scholar-official society, with hybrid forms such as ban-chin-yeong (half chin-yeong) coexisting alongside it for generations. What the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries brought was not the invention of the practice but its consolidation.

Under the settled pattern, a woman moved at marriage into her husband's household and lived thereafter under the authority of her parents-in-law (Sijip-sali). She came to be described in the common idiom as Chulga-oein (出嫁外人)—literally, "a person who has left the household and become an outsider." This was a proverb, not a legal status; but proverbs of this kind do a great deal of work. Dropping out of her natal family's property division and living inside her husband's, her position in that household was heavily dependent on her ability to produce a male heir, which placed acute pressure on female reproduction. The pressure had normative backing in the doctrine of the seven grounds for repudiating a wife (chilgeojiak), which listed childlessness among them, though the countervailing three grounds for non-repudiation (sambulgeo) and the availability of adoption both softened the absoluteness of the rule in practice.

- The Ritual Division of Labor

Ancestral veneration rites (Jesa and Charye) formed the core moral and spiritual duty of Confucian family life. However, these rituals functioned through a deeply asymmetric gendered division of labor:

Male Authority: Men officiated the public proceedings, read the ceremonial elegies, bowed before the ancestral tablets, and claimed the spiritual and social prestige of honoring the lineage.

Female Labor: Women, led by the eldest daughter-in-law of the main line (Jongbu), were tasked with the exhausting physical labor of preparing the codified ceremonial food offerings—a burden that in prominent lineages stretched across several days. The number and composition of the offerings varied considerably by lineage, region, and type of rite.

This structural separation—public prestige for men, unpaid domestic labor for women—remained deeply embedded in the Korean domestic consciousness. It is frequently identified as the cultural root of modern holiday conflicts (the phenomenon Koreans call Myeongjeol Syndrome) and of intergenerational marital tension well into the 21st century. That causal link is an interpretation rather than a documented chain, and readers should weigh it against the contemporary time-use evidence presented later in this book (see Figure 8), which measures how household and holiday labor is actually distributed today.

### 1.5 Filial Piety (Hyo) as Political Architecture: Isomorphism of Family and State

In the Neo-Confucian governance of Joseon, Filial Piety (Hyo, 孝) was far more than an ethical guideline for personal character; it was the foundational political philosophy of state sovereignty.

- The Isomorphism of Family and State

Neo-Confucian political theory constructed the state as an expanded family and the family as a miniature state. The structure is explicit in the Three Bonds (Samgang, 三綱), which pair the ruler's authority over the subject with the father's authority over the son:

The absolute authority of the father over the household was framed as a direct reflection of the absolute authority of the King over his subjects.

Devotion to parents (Hyo) served as the primary psychological training ground for unconditional political loyalty (Chung, 忠) to the monarch.

The legal architecture reinforced this. Joseon's *Gyeongguk Daejeon* delegated general criminal law to the Great Ming Code (Daemyeongnyul, 大明律), under which unfilial conduct (Bulhyo) was one of the Ten Abominations (Sibak)—the category of offenses excluded from amnesty. The *Gyeongguk Daejeon*'s own Punishments section functioned more as special legislation, concentrating on status, officialdom, and breaches of the governing order.

Punishments were graded rather than uniform. The unfilial category covered a wide range of conduct—neglecting to support one's parents, establishing a separate household and separate property while they lived, marrying during mourning for them—and drew correspondingly varied penalties, from beating to penal servitude to exile. The gravest offenses against parents, falling under the separate heading of monstrous depravity (Agyeok), carried capital punishment.

- Familial Collectivism vs. Modern Individualism

Unlike modern Western legal philosophies that position the autonomous individual with inalienable natural rights as the basic building block of society, traditional Korean culture positioned the patrilineal descent group (Munjung, 門中) as the fundamental social atom:

Shared Collective Responsibility: Personal achievements or failures were never evaluated in isolation; they elevated or disgraced the reputation of the entire lineage.

Intergenerational Reciprocity: Parents were expected to make extreme, sacrificial investments in their children's social advancement. In reciprocal return, adult children were bound by a moral obligation to support their parents in old age—physically, emotionally, and financially.

This last point is a statement about normative expectation, not about compliance. Whether that expectation was met, how it was renegotiated once the state began underwriting elder care, and what happened when it broke down are empirical questions, and this book takes them up in Chapter 5 rather than here.

This collectivistic ethic provided a resilient survival mechanism through centuries of foreign invasions, poverty, and state collapse. However, when this framework collided with the hyper-meritocratic, urbanized capitalist economy of the late 20th and early 21st centuries, it created acute structural gridlock—setting the stage for the dramatic legal, labor, and demographic transformations examined in the chapters that follow.

## 1.6 Evidence, variation, and the limits of the historical model

The historical argument requires a qualification. Early Joseon records showing daughters' inheritance, maternal kin connections, or residence near the wife's family do not demonstrate that gender equality existed in a modern sense. Social rank, household wealth, region, marital status, and the distinction between formal entitlement and actual possession all mattered. Nor did the later consolidation of patrilineal norms occur at one identical moment for every community. The safest interpretation is therefore one of directional transformation: over several centuries, a more flexible kinship field was increasingly organized around agnatic descent, male ritual leadership, and the reproduction of the household line.

This qualification strengthens rather than weakens the argument. It prevents two opposite errors. The first is to project the late Joseon model backward and imagine that all Korean families had always been organized by the same patriarchal rules. The second is to romanticize earlier arrangements as an egalitarian golden age. Historical households were capable of giving daughters property while still arranging marriages, labor, and ritual obligations through gendered hierarchies. The analytical question is not whether one period was simply "free" and another "oppressive," but which rights, duties, and resources were attached to which relationships.
