# CHAPTER 6: Generational Fractures & Toward an Adaptive Social Contract

### 6.1 Deconstructing the Friction Points

The Asymmetry of Military Service and Career Entry: For young men, mandatory military service (18 months in the Army and Marine Corps, 20 months in the Navy, and 21 months in the Air Force and in alternative social service) interrupts their early twenties, delaying university graduation and formal labor market entry. In a hyper-competitive economy, many young men perceive this as a structural disadvantage, and that perception is sharpest when they compare themselves with female peers who enter the labor market without interruption. Survey evidence shows how unevenly the obligation is read. In a Korean Women's Development Institute survey of 3,000 men aged 19 to 59, 72.2 percent of men in their twenties agreed that conscripting only men amounts to discrimination. In Hankook Research's gender-perception surveys, 68 percent of men in their twenties and 75 percent of men in their thirties said military service is not adequately compensated by society, compared with 25 percent and 32 percent of women in the same age brackets. What makes the issue politically combustible is less the length of service than this gap in how the same obligation is perceived. The Threat of the Domestic Trap: For young women, extensive educational investment fosters a strong professional identity. Career interruption is itself a measured phenomenon rather than an impression. Statistics Korea counts "career-interrupted women" (경력단절여성, *gyeongnyeok danjeol yeoseong*, commonly shortened in everyday speech to *gyeongdan*) as non-employed married women aged 15 to 54 who left or changed jobs because of marriage, pregnancy and childbirth, childcare, children's education, or family care. As of 2022 the stated reasons were childcare 42.8 percent, marriage 26.3 percent, pregnancy and childbirth 22.7 percent, family care 4.6 percent, and children's education 3.6 percent. A widely cited reading—and the one this book adopts—is that watching older female cohorts pass through this sequence, together with a disproportionate domestic care burden, encourages caution toward entering traditional family arrangements. The causal claim should be stated carefully: marriage timing is also shaped by housing costs, employment insecurity, and the cost of raising children, which Chapter 5 treats as the primary channel.

The Clash of Unfulfilled Expectations: Young men frequently report pressure to fulfill traditional male roles—above all, providing the primary housing capital for marriage. A survey of 1,000 recently married couples conducted by the matchmaking company Duo found that men covered 62.5 percent of housing-related marriage costs and women 37.5 percent, with housing accounting for the largest single share of total wedding expenditure. That figure should be read with caution, since the sample is drawn from a commercial matchmaking clientele rather than a nationally representative frame, and Chapter 5 notes that *honsu* practice has been shifting toward shared contributions. Young women, meanwhile, report being expected to maintain high professional standards while continuing to manage the domestic sphere. This book reads the resulting mismatch as a form of cultural lag: the obligations attached to older gender roles have outlasted the social deference that once accompanied them. The mismatch coincides with, and helps explain, unusually high levels of reported friction—in a 2022 Hankook Research survey, 90 percent of respondents in their twenties described gender conflict in Korea as serious, 15 percentage points higher than the previous year. Whether the structural mismatch *produces* that tension is a causal question survey data alone cannot settle.

### 6.2 The Intergenerational Perception Chasm: Industrial Generation vs. MZ Cohorts

The societal debate over family and gender is further complicated by a profound generational divide. Older cohorts who engineered South Korea's rapid post-war economic growth tend to evaluate family life through a lens shaped by mid-20th-century hardship, whereas younger urban generations more often prioritize personal autonomy, transparent merit, and mental well-being. Korean public discourse groups the latter under the label "MZ generation"—a Korean media coinage bundling Millennials and Generation Z into a single cohort. Readers outside Korea should note that this grouping is rarely used elsewhere: international demography normally treats Millennials (born roughly 1981–1996) and Gen Z (born roughly 1997–2012) as distinct cohorts with different formative conditions. The term is used here because it structures the Korean debate, not because it is a standard analytical category. Generational descriptions of this kind are also generalizations; they characterize tendencies, not individuals.

The Disconnect in Social Norms

Communication Breakdown (Kkondae Discourse): Older family members often frame questions about marital status, childbearing, or career plans as expressions of relational care and warmth—*jeong* (정, 情), the dense affective bond of obligation and attachment that Koreans describe as accumulating between people who are close. Younger generations frequently interpret the same inquiries as boundary violations, unwarranted surveillance, and outdated social pressure, and the word *kkondae*—a slang term for a condescending, lecturing elder that entered mainstream Korean usage around 2019—has become the shorthand for that reading. [NEEDS RESEARCH: survey data on younger Koreans' reported stress at holiday family gatherings, to move this from authorial observation to documented finding.]

Re-Evaluating Sacrifice: This book interprets the generational shift less as a decline of family feeling than as a change in the perceived rationality of sacrifice. The industrial generation was more likely to treat individual sacrifice for the family as a high moral virtue, in a period when such sacrifice was visibly repaid by rising household prosperity. Younger Koreans, confronting a hyper-competitive economy and high housing costs, are more likely to weigh uncompensated sacrifice against the security it actually delivers. This contrast is presented as an analytical reading, not as a measured finding. ### 6.3 Expanding the Domestic Sphere: De Facto Unions, Single Parenthood, and Civil Partnerships

Marriage in South Korea fell sharply over three decades—the crude marriage rate dropped from 9.6 per 1,000 people in 1990 into the three-to-four range through the 2010s—but the most recent data are not a continuation of that slide. According to Statistics Korea's 2024 marriage and divorce statistics, there were 222,000 marriages in 2024, an increase of 29,000, or 14.8 percent, over the previous year. That is the largest absolute increase since 1996 and the largest rate of increase since the series began in 1970, lifting the crude marriage rate to 4.4 and producing the highest annual total since 2019. Births moved in the same direction, with 254.5 thousand live births recorded in the preliminary 2025 release, 6.8 percent above 2024.[2] The long-term shift away from marriage-centered household formation has not reversed, and it is too early to say whether the upturn reflects a durable change or the delayed completion of marriages postponed during the pandemic years. But any account of Korean family change written now must distinguish the structural trend from this recent rebound rather than treat decline as self-evidently ongoing.

What is not in doubt is that the legal and social definition of "family" is under pressure to expand. Korean citizens are forming diverse cohabitation arrangements that the historically rigid, marriage-centered definition of the domestic unit does not accommodate.

Institutional Lag and Legal Debates

While non-traditional households are increasingly common in urban centers like Seoul, South Korea's legal and welfare frameworks remain largely anchored to marriage-based family units:

The Framework Act on Healthy Families (건강가정기본법): Article 3 still defines "family" as a basic social unit formed through marriage, blood ties, or adoption, and that definition remains in force today. It is worth being precise about the direction of travel. In 2021 the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family adopted revision of the definition as a policy task in the Fourth Basic Plan for Healthy Families (2021–2025), and bills were introduced in the 21st National Assembly to delete the definitional clause and replace "healthy family" with neutral language such as "family support" and "family policy." Those bills lapsed amid organized opposition and a change of governmental position, so the marriage-centered definition was not narrowed but retained. English-language readers should not read "historically defined" into this: the attempt to change the definition failed, which is precisely why it illustrates institutional lag.

The Framework Act sets the tone, but the operative exclusions are produced by specific statutes rather than by the framework law itself, which is largely declaratory. Housing is one example: under the special-supply rules for newlywed couples, eligibility is tied to a registered marriage of no more than seven years as evidenced by the marriage relation certificate, which excludes unmarried cohabiting couples outright. End-of-life and emergency medical decisions are another: the Act on Life-Sustaining Treatment Decisions limits "patient's family" to a spouse aged 19 or over and direct ascendants and descendants within one degree of kinship (extending to two degrees, and then to siblings, only in their absence), a definition that tracks the Civil Act's Article 779 concept of family and leaves an unmarried partner without standing. Keeping these levels distinct—framework declaration, operative statute, administrative rule—matters for the argument this chapter makes in 6.5.

Health insurance is the instructive exception, and it points in the opposite direction from the other two. Dependant registration under the National Health Insurance system has recognized de facto (common-law) opposite-sex spouses, and on 18 July 2024 the Supreme Court, sitting en banc, held by nine votes to four that excluding a same-sex partner from dependant status was unlawful: the National Health Insurance Act contains no express provision excluding same-sex partners, and exclusion on that ground alone constitutes discrimination based on sexual orientation contrary to the constitutional principle of equality. The National Health Insurance Service subsequently registered the plaintiff as his partner's dependant, and the National Human Rights Commission issued a statement welcoming the decision. The pattern echoes the 2005 Constitutional Court decision on the *hoju* system discussed in Chapter 3: in Korean family law, judicial reasoning has repeatedly opened the door before the legislature has walked through it.

The Living Together Guarantee Act Movements (Civil Partnership Debates): Legislative proposals inspired by civil pact frameworks—above all France's PACS (Pacte civil de solidarité), introduced in 1999, which allows opposite-sex and same-sex couples to register at the town hall and acquire mutual support obligations along with inheritance, tax, social-security, and labor-related entitlements—have been introduced in the National Assembly. The decisive fact is that none has ever passed. Representative Yong Hye-in of the Basic Income Party filed the first Living Together Partnership bill ever formally introduced in the National Assembly in April 2023, with eleven co-sponsoring members; Representative Jang Hye-young of the Justice Party introduced a separate package of three family-formation-rights bills the same year. Both lapsed without deliberation when the 21st Assembly's term expired in May 2024. A new bill was reintroduced by Representative Yong on 3 September 2025 in the 22nd Assembly, extending rights and duties comparable to those of marriage to adults who agree to share and support each other's lives, and it remains pending. Because the bill does not condition eligibility on the partners' sexes, it would cover same-sex couples. Beyond that, this book describes the proposals as extending rights broadly analogous to those currently bundled into marriage, rather than enumerating specific entitlements, because several commonly cited effects would require parallel amendments to tax and medical legislation. ### 6.4 Conclusion: Building an Adaptive, Sustainable Social Contract

As South Korea navigates the 2020s and beyond, the central sociological argument of Book 2 comes into focus: low fertility, gender conflict, and the decline of marriage-based household formation are better read as a structural call for institutional adaptation than as evidence of moral decay. The wording matters here. This is not a story of families breaking apart in the conventional sense—the crude divorce rate peaked at 3.4 per 1,000 in 2003 and has declined since, standing at 2.2 in 2023 and 1.8 in 2024, with roughly 91,000 divorces in 2024. What has changed is the size and formation of households, not their stability once formed: single-person households rose from 9.0 percent of all households in 1990 to 35.5 percent in 2023.[1] Popular accounts that describe the situation as hedonistic Westernization have the mechanism backwards.

The Confucian legacy is often credited with giving South Korea an extraordinary foundation of educational commitment, social cohesion, and resilience during its rise from post-war poverty to an advanced industrial democracy. The causal weight of that legacy remains contested in the scholarship, and this book has argued throughout against treating Confucianism as a static cultural essence that explains outcomes on its own; the point here is narrower, namely that the institutional forms Korea built around family obligation proved highly effective under one set of economic conditions. Sustaining the country under a different set requires updating that social operating system to align with democratic values, individual autonomy, and genuine gender equality.

By dismantling lingering structural barriers in the workplace, closing the implementation gap for parental leave analyzed in Chapter 4, expanding legal protections for diverse family structures, and establishing genuine partnership within domestic life, South Korea has the capacity to transform its current transitional friction into a more sustainable, inclusive, and resilient future.

## 6.5 From gender conflict to institutional bargaining

Public discussion often describes young men and young women as opposing camps. A more precise analysis treats gender conflict as a dispute over the allocation of risk, recognition, and obligation. Men may describe military service, housing costs, and expectations of economic provision. Women may describe safety, career interruption, unpaid care, and the expectation to manage family logistics. These concerns are not identical, and neither side’s account should be used to erase the other. They become politically explosive when institutions distribute the costs of adulthood through gendered assumptions.

The task is not to produce a symmetrical equation in which every disadvantage is declared equal. It is to identify where disadvantages originate, who bears them, and which policy can change them. Military service is a state obligation; housing scarcity is a market and policy problem; career interruption is shaped by workplace design and care provision; domestic inequality is negotiated within households but reinforced by wider institutions. Keeping these levels separate makes constructive bargaining more possible.

## 6.6 Legal pluralism and the test of inclusion

Expanding recognition of family diversity requires more than adding a new label to a form. It requires decisions about inheritance, taxation, housing, medical consent, social insurance, parental status, domestic violence protection, and the termination of a relationship. Marriage currently bundles many of these rights into one institution. A civil partnership or cohabitation framework would need to specify which rights are automatic, which require registration, and how vulnerable partners are protected when a relationship ends.

The policy debate should also avoid a false choice between “traditional family” and unlimited individual choice. Many people want durable care relationships, but they may not be able or willing to enter marriage. A pluralistic framework can protect stability without prescribing one household form. The relevant standard is whether people who share care, residence, economic responsibility, or dependency can obtain fair protection, while children and vulnerable adults receive priority.

## 6.7 A policy evaluation framework

The final question is not whether Korea can return to an earlier family model. It is whether institutions can make care, work, and belonging compatible with democratic equality. Four tests are useful. The first is access: can people obtain support regardless of marital status, gender, region, or employment type? The second is time: do policies give adults enough predictable time to care? The third is security: do family choices threaten housing, income, health insurance, or later-life protection? The fourth is voice: can individuals contest decisions made by employers, relatives, welfare agencies, and courts?

These tests produce a more realistic social contract than a promise to restore fertility through incentives alone. This book's assessment is that birth subsidies may help at the margin but cannot compensate for insecure work, unaffordable housing, unequal care, or social stigma. The judgment should be stated as a judgment, because the evidence is genuinely contested: several Korean research institutes have found the fertility effects of cash transfers to be limited, while officials at Statistics Korea have cited government marriage-promotion policy as one factor behind the 2024 marriage rebound described in 6.3. The rebound is recent enough that its causes cannot yet be attributed with confidence, and readers should treat both the skeptical and the favorable reading of incentive policy as open.
