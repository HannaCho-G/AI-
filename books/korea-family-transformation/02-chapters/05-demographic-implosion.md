In contemporary South Korea, this traditional imperative has eroded. Driven by rapid urban concentration, escalating living costs, and changing personal values, young Koreans increasingly view marriage through the lens of individual autonomy, financial feasibility, and risk management rather than moral duty.

The Economic Threshold of Marriage
The postponement and avoidance of marriage in modern South Korea is directly linked to the high upfront capital requirements of household formation:

The Housing Capital Hurdle: Since the second half of the twentieth century, entering marriage in South Korea has generally been understood to require securing an independent home, typically through the Jeonse (전세, lump-sum deposit lease) system or an outright property purchase. It should be noted that the nationwide spread of Jeonse is a modern housing practice rather than a Confucian inheritance, and that its share of the rental market has been contracting since the deposit-fraud and reverse-Jeonse disruptions of 2022–2023, with monthly rent (Wolse, 월세) expanding in its place. With metropolitan apartment prices rising in the Greater Seoul Area, young adults face capital requirements that are widely reported to exceed what early career savings can cover. ### 5.2 The Sustained Growth of Single-Person Households (Honjok Culture)

The re-evaluation of marriage is physically reflected in the transformation of South Korea's residential landscape: the sustained rise of Single-Person Households (1인 가구) and the normalization of solo living, sociologically captured by the term Honjok (혼족, The Solo Tribe).

The scale of this shift is best stated with figures rather than adjectives. Single-person households accounted for 9.0% of all households in 1990 (1,021 thousand households), 15.5% in 2000, 23.9% in 2010, 27.2% in 2015, 35.5% in 2023 (7,829 thousand households), and 36.1% in 2024 (8,045 thousand households) — the highest share on record (Statistics Korea, Population and Housing Census; 2024 figure from the register-based census). Two points follow from this series. First, the denominator is households, not dwellings: roughly one in every 2.8 Korean households now consists of a single person. Second, the trend is a steady, sustained increase of roughly seven to eight percentage points per decade rather than an exponential acceleration; the change is remarkable because it has been unbroken for three and a half decades, not because it has suddenly spiked.

Deconstructing the Honjok Phenomenon
Practices once stigmatized as socially isolating have been rebranded into accepted, commercialized expressions of autonomy and personal lifestyle preference:

Honbap (혼밥): Dining alone in restaurants, accompanied by the visible spread of single-seat partitions, automated kiosk ordering, and individual hot-pot and barbecue setups across urban centers. [NEEDS RESEARCH: 1인 외식 인프라 확산을 뒷받침할 소비·외식업 통계 또는 정책 문헌 1건. 현재 이 서술은 관찰에 근거하며 인용 가능한 출처가 원고·부록 어디에도 없다.]

Honsul (혼술): Consuming alcohol alone at home or in quiet, specialized single-patron bars — often interpreted as a reaction against hierarchical corporate drinking gatherings (Hoesik, 회식), though the causal link between the decline of Hoesik and the rise of solo drinking has not been established by a specific study.

Honhaeng (혼행, also written 혼여행): Traveling solo to prioritize personal rest and reflection over group itineraries.

The growth of single-person households cannot be attributed purely to ideological individualism. It represents an intersection where rising personal autonomy meets structural economic headwinds and urban infrastructure optimized for solitary convenience. This is the analysis advanced in this book rather than a claim attributed to unnamed researchers, and Section 5.5 sets out the demographic evidence for it — including the fact that the largest single-person household group in Korea is no longer young adults.

### 5.3 Deconstructing the Lowest Total Fertility Rate in the OECD

The convergence of career interruption risks, delayed marriage, escalating living costs, and intense competition has produced South Korea's most pressing demographic crisis: a Total Fertility Rate (TFR) that dropped to 0.72 in 2023 (0.721 in the finalized series), rose modestly to 0.75 in 2024 (0.748 finalized), and recovered to 0.80 in 2025 — the first return to the 0.8 range in four years, confirmed by the finalized 2025 birth statistics released in August 2026 (254,300 live births, up 6.7% from 2024). All three figures stand far below the population replacement level of 2.1, and far below the OECD average of 1.40 in 2024.

A note on the superlative: Korea has the lowest fertility rate among OECD member countries, and this remains true in the most recent comparable year. It is not, however, the lowest rate in the world at any given moment. Macao recorded roughly 0.68 in 2024, below Korea's 0.75, and Hong Kong has been at a comparable or lower level; reporting since early 2026 indicates that Taiwan's 2025 rate fell below Korea's 0.80 as Korea rebounded for a second consecutive year. Macao, Hong Kong, and Taiwan are not OECD members, so they fall outside the comparison used here. Readers encountering the widely circulated phrase "the world's lowest birth rate" should therefore read it together with a reference year and a defined comparison group — the same discipline this book applies to every other statistic it cites.

Sociological Frameworks of the Demographic Shift
The Rational Investment Framing: This book uses "rational investment" as a descriptive framing rather than an established technical term; it draws on the quantity–quality tradeoff familiar from family economics. Rather than reflecting an aversion to parenthood, low fertility on this reading operates as a calculated decision by prospective parents responding to severe competitive pressures. When the perceived financial and emotional investment required to raise a successful child in modern Korea is exceptionally high, individuals choose to limit family size to one child or forgo parenting entirely.

Private Education Pressure (Hagwon Costs): Korean parents devote a substantial share of monthly household income to private tutoring and after-school academies (Hagwon, 학원) so that their children remain competitive in the university entrance examination (Suneung). The scale is documented rather than impressionistic. According to the 2024 Survey of Private Education Expenditures on Elementary, Middle and High School Students (Statistics Korea and the Ministry of Education, released March 2025), total national spending on private education reached 29.2 trillion won, up 7.7% from the previous year. Averaged across all students — including those receiving no tutoring at all — monthly spending was 474,000 won per student, an increase of 9.3% in a single year; among students who actually participated, the monthly average was 592,000 won. The participation rate reached 80.0%, up 1.5 percentage points. In other words, four out of five Korean schoolchildren are enrolled in paid private education, and the cost of that norm is now a standing line item in the household budget of a prospective parent. The perceived necessity of these investments turns child-rearing into an expensive enterprise long before a child reaches university.

Metropolitan Hyper-Concentration: More than half of South Korea's total population resides within the Greater Seoul Metropolitan Area — 26,047,523 of 51,217,221 residents, or 50.86%, in 2024 (Ministry of the Interior and Safety, resident registration statistics); the capital region first crossed the 50% threshold in November 2019. This intense geographic concentration fuels competition for housing, prestigious schools, and primary-tier corporate jobs, and is associated with lower fertility in metropolitan areas, where Seoul records the lowest rate of any province-level jurisdiction. The direction of causation is not settled: age structure, marriage rates, housing costs, and the selective migration of young unmarried people into the capital region all operate at once, so concentration is best read as one component of a cluster of pressures rather than a single direct cause.

### 5.4 The Ethics of Elder Care (Hyo in Transition): From Domestic Labor to Social Insurance

The transformation of the family unit has also restructured the Confucian ethic of filial piety (Hyo, 孝) regarding elder care.

The Shift to Institutional and Public Care
From the late Joseon period through the twentieth century, co-residing with elderly parents and providing direct hands-on care was framed as the duty of the eldest son — and, in practice, of his wife. Today, this dynamic has shifted toward institutionalized care and independent living:

Decline in Co-Residence: The proportion of older people living in the same household as their adult children has fallen sharply, and the recent decline has been abrupt rather than gradual. The Survey of Living Conditions of Older Persons (Ministry of Health and Welfare) records co-residence with children at 20.1% in 2020 and 10.3% in 2023 — a halving within three years — while the share of older people living alone rose to 32.8%, up 13.0 percentage points from 2020. Preference data point the same way: in the 2023 survey, only 2.5% of respondents said they would prefer to live with family while in good health, and a further 4.3% wished to live near family, meaning the overwhelming majority prefer independent living as long as their physical health permits.

National Long-Term Care Insurance (노인장기요양보험): Implemented in July 2008, this statutory social insurance program formalized elder care as a shared public responsibility rather than an exclusively private, unpaid burden carried by female family members.

The Redefinition of Filial Duty: In contemporary Korea, filial piety is expressed less through continuous physical co-habitation and more through visits, digital communication, advocacy within medical systems, and — in some households — financial contributions and the arranging of professional care. It would be a mistake, however, to describe this as money simply replacing co-residence. The decline in co-residence is well documented; a corresponding rise in regular financial support from children is not. Korea's old-age poverty rate remains among the highest in the OECD, and the share of private transfers from children in older people's income has trended downward over the long term while public transfers have grown. What has changed most clearly is the institutional location of care, not the generosity of intergenerational cash flows. [NEEDS RESEARCH: 노인 소득 중 사적이전소득 비중 시계열 확정 수치(노인실태조사 또는 가계금융복지조사, 기준연도 명시) — 이 문단의 인과 서술을 확정하려면 필요.]

This transition highlights South Korea's broader societal trajectory: the shift from relying exclusively on informal family networks to developing institutionalized social safety nets.

## 5.5 Demography, housing, and the timing of adulthood

Low fertility should not be explained as a single preference variable. It is an outcome produced by the timing of adulthood, the cost of independent housing, the stability of employment, the organization of education, and the perceived consequences of becoming a parent. Marriage remains important because births continue to be strongly associated with marriage in Korea — births outside marriage account for only about 4 to 5% of all births in recent years, among the lowest shares in the OECD — even as attitudes toward marriage become more conditional. When marriage is delayed, births are delayed as well; when marriage requires a home, a stable job, and expensive educational expectations, the threshold for family formation rises. The private education figures cited above are one measure of that threshold: a household contemplating a child is also contemplating a monthly obligation that four out of five Korean families already carry.

The rise of one-person households has a related but distinct meaning. Some reflect young adults' transition before marriage; others reflect later-life widowhood, divorce, non-marriage, or independent living among older people. The 2024 register-based census makes this concrete: by age, the largest group of single-person households is now those aged 70 and over at 19.8%, which in 2024 overtook the under-29 group at 17.8% for the first time, followed by people in their sixties at 17.6% and in their thirties at 17.4%. The typical Korean living alone is, in other words, no longer best pictured as a young urban professional. Treating all one-person households as "honjok culture" therefore risks turning a demographic category into a lifestyle stereotype — and the commercialized solo-living imagery described in Section 5.2 describes only one segment of the category.

Both sides deserve to be shown. Solo living carries real autonomy and convenience; it also carries income, health, loneliness, and care risks that fall unevenly. The 2023 Survey of Living Conditions of Older Persons found that older people living alone reported markedly worse outcomes than those living as couples: 34.2% rated their own health as good, against 48.6% among older couples; 16.1% showed depressive symptoms, against 7.8%; and 73.9% reported difficulties in daily living, against 48.1%. The fastest-growing part of Korea's one-person household population is also its most vulnerable part.

## 5.6 Care, ageing, and the public meaning of filial piety

The transformation of elder care is not a simple replacement of family by the state. Public long-term care insurance, hospitals, local welfare centers, home-care services, and private facilities now form a mixed system in which families still coordinate decisions, finance gaps, and provide emotional support. Daughters, daughters-in-law, spouses, and sons may all be involved, but their responsibilities are not distributed evenly — the time-use evidence discussed in Chapter 4 shows the same asymmetry in the earlier stages of the family life course. Institutional care can reduce the physical burden of co-residence while creating new questions about affordability, quality, and access. [NEEDS RESEARCH: 주 수발자 성별 구성 수치(노인실태조사) 또는 장기요양보험 통계연보 1건 — 이 절의 젠더 비대칭 서술을 수치로 뒷받침하기 위해 필요.]

Filial piety therefore changes meaning rather than disappears. It can involve visits, financial contributions, advocacy within medical systems, digital communication, or arranging professional care. The ethical issue is whether these expectations are voluntary and shared, or whether they are enforced through gendered guilt and inadequate public provision. A democratic welfare state should not abolish intergenerational responsibility; it should prevent responsibility from becoming an unpriced private tax on one category of family member.
